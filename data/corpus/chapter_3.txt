3 BugBook: Ecotoxicology, hazard, and safety in insects 

Abstract

This BugBook article provides an overview of edible insect toxicological concerns, focusing on exposure routes, mechanisms of toxicity, and assessment endpoints relevant to human and environmental health. Insects are exposed to contaminants through dermal, oral, injection, and inhalation routes. This article explores key toxicity mechanisms, including neurotoxicity, endocrine disruption, oxidative stress, and immune suppression, emphasising their implications for toxicity in insects. Methodological approaches to assess the toxicity of contaminants, such as topical application, dietary exposures, bioaccumulation assays, and advanced modelling techniques, are discussed, highlighting their adaptability for edible insect species. Standardised protocols, such as those from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) and the International Organisation for Standardisation (ISO), are presented whenever possible (excluding bees). Data analyses, interpretation, and modelling are briefly summarised, along with examples and case studies that highlight the relevance of toxicity testing for ensuring food safety, regulatory compliance, and sustainable insect farming practices. Bioaccumulation studies using toxicokinetics modelling reveal insights into contaminant uptake and elimination in relevant edible insect species, supporting practical applications like depuration periods in rearing facilities. By addressing current gaps in methodologies and providing a roadmap for ecotoxicological research, this article aims to guide policymakers, researchers, and industry stakeholders in promoting safe and sustainable practices for the insect-based food and feed sector. The findings underscore the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration in harmonising global standards, safeguarding public health, and advancing circular bioeconomy goals.

1 Context

Insects play different and crucial roles in ecosystem services, such as pollination and nutrient recycling, contributing to the ecological balance. However, habitat loss, climate change, and exposure to synthetic chemical contaminants, including agrochemicals and industrial pollutants, pose significant threats to insect populations. In fact, their response to stressors can serve as a warning to early environmental changes that make them valuable bioindicators (Chowdhury et al., 2023; Kevan, 1999) and helpful model organisms (Koivula, 2011). Their life cycles are typically short with rapid maturation, quickly producing new generations of offspring that can help measure the immediate consequences of contaminants and predict the longer-term impacts on their populations and ecosystem function, structure and services. These studies, however, reveal the resilience and vulnerabilities of insect populations and often represent early warning signals for more significant ecological shifts that could ultimately impact other species – including humans.

Insects are also important human food items, traditionally consumed in many parts of Asia, Africa, and Southern America. In the last decades, there has been a growing interest from the rest of the world, including Europe, for insects as an additional protein source to human and animal diets, among other novel applications such as waste recycling, bioconversion, and using non-edible components in green chemistry (Orkusz, 2021; Rehman et al., 2023). As we approach the 10 billion mark for the global population in 2050, urgent protein-rich food alternatives will be needed that can be produced from complementary and environmentally friendly sources (United Nations, 2024). Insect farming provides more sustainable options because insects are easy to farm, have a high nutritional content, and present a low environmental footprint (Tabassum-Abbasi et al., 2016; van Huis and Oonincx, 2017). However, the growing consumption of insects raises questions regarding food safety. The circular perspective, where insects can convert low-quality substrates into high-quality protein (and other entomo-based co-products) poses, on the other hand, the question of how insects cope with possible contaminants from those side streams (van Huis and Oonincx, 2017). Due to the a priori need for the safety of insect-based food chains, evaluating the toxicity of relevant contaminants to insects becomes a focal point for different stakeholders. Researchers focus on understanding the toxicity mechanisms and identifying the risks and gaps; producers’ main interest is the practical application of knowledge for optimisation and safety in the rearing facilities, and food protection authorities require toxicity profiling and weight of evidence from scientific research for approval of novel foods. Ultimately, consumers prioritise foods rich in protein and containing beneficial lipid profiles, along with a safety certification.

This article starts with an overview of the principles of toxicity testing and its importance in protecting the health of humans, animals, and ecosystems. Exposure routes and how chemical substances reach insects are essential to setting adequate experimental designs and fulfilling the research aim. With the obtained results, one can infer how insect physiology is affected in a multifaceted manner through the disruption of the nervous system and hormonal pathways, or by inducing oxidative stress due to exposure to contaminants. The methodologies used for these tests are of different natures and complexity, thus pointing towards the challenging aspects of toxicological research. Several knowledge gaps have been identified, such as an evident lack of clear protocols and specific methodologies related to the ecotoxicity of edible insects. Therefore, some examples provided are commonly used in ecotoxicological approaches with other insect groups (excluding honeybees and other pollinators). In organisations like the International Organisation for Standardisation (ISO) or the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), guidance documents, technical guidelines, and standards regarding insects are scarce. One of the few available is the OECD Guidance Document 122 (2010) on dung beetle toxicity testing. Dung beetle larvae (Aphodius constans) are exposed to spiked dung, focusing on veterinary pharmaceutical substances. A. constans first instar larvae (up to 7 d old) are used in this test, and the number of surviving larvae and morphological changes (e.g., abnormalities, size, biomass) are recorded after three weeks of exposure. Additionally, the OECD 228 (2008) outlines a method to assess the developmental toxicity of chemicals on dung-dwelling stages of dipteran species by mixing the chemical with bovine faeces and monitoring effects on development, emergence, and morphology in test species under controlled conditions. ISO 20963 (2005) standard was developed to depict the lethal effects of pollutants on the insect larvae Oxythyrea funesta, which are exposed through cuticular or food ingestion, or even using contaminated soils.

Then, protocols for different testing methodologies (e.g., topical applications, dietary exposures, and bioaccumulation assays) are described and are intended to guide future experimental designs and adaptations, as many examples presented outside the edible insect groups can be easily applied. In addition, this article provides insight into statistical analysis and modelling of toxicity data from standardised and common ones used in ecotoxicology, to those that can be applied to more complex sets of experimental data and how these tools can help translate the obtained results to real-world scenarios. Briefly presented case studies show how toxicity testing with edible insects can be applied to related environmental concerns.

The overarching aim of this article is to serve as a roadmap for edible insect ecotoxicological research, with its direct implications for policies, environmental management, animal and human health, and circular bioeconomy. Inevitably, considering the current environmental degradation and food shortages, and given the vital role of insects on our planet, the knowledge of how contaminants affect insects is paramount. The article reviews the background of insect toxicity testing and provides a perspective that can serve a more sustainable future and protect ecosystems, wildlife, livestock, and human populations.
2 Exposure routes, modes of action, and endpoints in ecotoxicity tests

Understanding how contaminants affect insects requires a detailed exploration of how these substances enter the insect’s body, the biological pathways they impair, and the specific outcomes or endpoints that indicate toxicity (Kopit and Pitts-Singer, 2018). This section delves into the various exposure routes of contaminants, the mechanisms by which they exert their toxic effects, and the key endpoints commonly used to assess their impact on insect health and life traits.

Exposure routes in toxicity tests

Due to their small size and diverse habitats, insects encounter contaminants through various exposure routes, significantly influencing their physiology and behaviour. The primary routes of exposure in in vivo toxicity tests include dermal, oral, respiratory, and injection exposure. In vitro testing using insect cell lines is a common tool in fundamental and applied research, although there is a limited number of reported works with edible insect species.

Dermal exposure: Insects can absorb many compounds through exposure to their exoskeleton or cuticle, which is a protective barrier to the external environment. The cuticle is the principal barrier that protects insects from becoming permeable to some chemical molecules due to their multilayered lipids, proteins, and chitin structure. The absorption rate of chemicals through the cuticle is influenced by several factors, including the insect’s morphology and the molecular size, solubility, and polarity of the chemicals involved (Johnson et al., 2012; Valdés et al., 2024). Insects exposed directly to contaminated surfaces, through plant leaves, soil, or water, can absorb these chemicals through body parts not protected by the cuticle, like the sensory structures and the lining of tracheoles, or even during moulting. Different life stages and sexes can also present possible variations for dermal exposure and sensitivities, observed for the edible insect yellow mealworm Tenebrio molitor, which can show different sensitivities under dermal exposure to agrochemicals, as demonstrated in the laboratory conditions (Naccarato et al., 2023).

Oral exposure: Occurs when insects ingest contaminated food (e.g., substrates, plants, or prey), soil particles, or water. Contaminated feed material is consumed, and chemicals can be metabolised, detoxified in the insect’s digestive system, or accumulated in insect bodies. The ability of insects to break down these contaminants varies depending on the species, type of contaminant, and concentration to which they are subjected. It is known that the gut microbiome of insects can play a role in the metabolism, immunoregulation, and degradation of toxins in the body of the host insect (Siddiqui et al., 2022). At the same time, the concepts of bioaccumulation and biomagnification should be addressed to understand how contaminants can be passed throughout the food chain. In light of the edible insect’s safety concerns, this is one of the most important routes of exposure regarding contaminants transition alongside the food chain, including the impact on humans (Cardoso et al., 2023, 2024; Truzzi et al., 2022). With the current need to increase the sustainability of using insects as circularity tools, the demand to find new feed sources for rearing insects is aligned with sustainable waste management practices. Therefore, advanced knowledge of insects’ bioaccumulation ability regarding different contaminants can largely contribute to using a more extensive range of bio-based residues (Cardoso et al., 2023, 2024).

Respiratory exposure: Once insects inhale contaminants, despite their respiratory system being entirely different from human lungs (a network of tracheae), they can suffer adverse effects from contaminants’ uptake. Insects have their way of breathing through the openings known as spiracles in their exoskeleton, which are connected to the tracheal system (Gillott, 2005). As air often contains fine particles, aerosols, or gaseous contaminants, these may also be inhaled through the spiracles into the tracheae. Once these contaminants enter the tracheal system and diffuse into surrounding tissues or hemolymph (insect blood), they can trigger multiple toxicodynamic processes, leading to toxic effects. These exposures are significant in highly industrialised and urban areas where high levels of pollution exist, as well as during the application of pesticides on agricultural lands (Kaushal et al., 2021).

Injection exposure: Injection exposure is usually related to introducing a specific known contaminant dose directly into the insect tissues or hemolymph stream. It is possible to analyse the toxic effects of the contaminant at a specific concentration and location within an insect’s body without considering external confounding variables, removing the bias of the insect’s behaviour in the uptake of contaminants. It also provides information on the dose response of different contaminants and their mode of action at the molecular and cellular levels, which is extremely important in ecotoxicological bioassays. Studies in the laboratory using injection methods can help refine risk assessments and toxicity thresholds for wild-caught insect exposures (Wu and Yi, 2016).

In vitro exposure: Insect cell lines are frequently used in pesticide development and management research. Most of these cell lines are developed from insect embryos, larvae, and ovaries and, to a lesser extent, from the midgut, nervous system, hemolymph, and fat body (Arrese and Soulages, 2010; He et al., 2023). Some of the many examples of the use of insect cell lines in toxicology research are the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster S2 (late-stage embryos), the midge Aedes albopictus C6/36 (embryonic origin), and the fall armyworm Spodoptera frugiperda Sf9 (pupal ovary tissue) (Cc et al., 2021 and the references therein). The cell lines Sf21 of S. frugiperda were applied to study the cytotoxicity of fungicides (propiconazole and CuSO4), demonstrating the viability and sensitivity of the cells and the adequacy of the approach for acute toxicity screening (Grigg et al., 2024). The reports on developing cell lines of the edible insect species officially approved for food and feed are scarce. In 1994, the cell lines from the fat body of the Tenebrio molitor beetle were developed to study the proteins relevant to low-temperature survival (Easton and Horwath, 1994). A recent report by Saathoff et al. (2024) on the development of Hermetia illucens cell lines BCIRL-HiE0122021-SGS from eggs opens the possibility of their application in areas of biotechnology of edible insects, toxicology, etc. (Saathoff et al., 2024).
Modes of action

This section lists the various modes of action under which contaminants can act on insects, including, but not limited to, inhibition of neural communication, immune suppression, and metabolic dysfunction. This is particularly important for assessing and understanding the overall effects of pollutants on insect populations and ecosystems. Because toxicity testing targeting these modes of action can ultimately enlighten the long-term ecological implications for insect exposures, strategies to reduce such environmental contamination and avert detrimental roles in modulating insect biodiversity may be predicted. The principal modes of action in insect toxicology are shown for some different classes.

Neurotoxicity: Insecticides, including organophosphates, pyrethroids, and neonicotinoids, bind to neural receptors and disrupt neurotransmission. One example is the case of organophosphates that block acetylcholinesterase (AChE), an enzyme that degrades the neurotransmitter acetylcholine at the synaptic cleft (Tsai and Lein, 2021). As a consequence, the acetylcholine accumulates and leads to endless nerve firing, with muscle overstimulation as seen in tremors or paralysis – the eventual outcome is death from respiratory failure due to shutdown of diaphragm muscles. Pyrethroids, another class of neurotoxic insecticides, disturb voltage-gated sodium channels in nerve cells, leading to protracted activity (Soderlund, 2020). Neonicotinoids, commonly used in agriculture, target insects’ nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) and cause fatal hyperexcitation (Anadón et al., 2020).

Endocrine disruptors: Endocrine disruptors can influence insect growth regulators (IGRs) in the hormonal systems, controlling insect development and reproduction (Crane et al., 2022). Juvenile hormones and ecdysteroids (a class of steroid hormones) are essential for insect moulting, metamorphosis, and reproduction. Endocrine disruptors can, for instance, interfere with the body’s hormonal messaging, mimicking or blocking natural hormones and their respective receptors within insect bodies, which can precede arrested development and sterility (Masner et al., 1968). Juvenile hormone analogues, a class that inhibits the progression between larval and adult stages, can also make larvae either become long-lived or never reach sexual maturity (Parthasarathy and Palli, 2021). Additional endocrine disruptors may affect the equilibrium of ecdysteroids, which govern moulting and set off rare or abnormal moulds. These disruptions may lead to decreased fertility, malformed offspring, and population losses. Endocrine disruption effects are usually subtle but can significantly impact insect populations, particularly in environments with chronic low-level exposure (Oehlmann and Schulte-Oehlmann, 2003).

Oxidative stress: Oxidative stress is a condition in which reactive oxygen species (ROS) can be generated more than their detoxification by an insect species. Exposure to some contaminants, such as metals, pesticides, and others, may activate cellular signalling pathways that increase the production of ROS in insect cells (Anetor et al., 2022). Indeed, these reactive species, primarily free radicals, tend to associate with cellular elements such as lipids, proteins, or DNA, which impose oxidative damage (Jomova et al., 2024). Oxidative stress-related damage can disrupt critical cell functions, often resulting in apoptosis or senescence. In insects, oxidative stress leads to behavioural changes, developmental defects in offspring, reduced reproductive success, and increased mortality (Anetor et al., 2022). Insects, like most species, have developed antioxidant defence systems (e.g., the enzymes superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, and glutathione peroxidases) to alleviate the effects of oxidative stress. However, high levels of ROS produced in the cells will challenge the insect’s antioxidant defence and lead to a net accumulation of oxidative damage. Therefore, adequate mechanistic understanding is crucial to assessing contaminants’ chronic toxicity and insect protective responses.

Immune system suppression: Some contaminants also lower the immune system of insects and, therefore, make them susceptible to pathogens, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites. The immune system of insects involves both humoral and cellular components that recognise pathogens (Vinson, 1993). Cellular immunity is ruled by immune cells known as hemocytes that phagocytose and kill pathogens; on the other hand, humoral immunity includes both antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) and various antimicrobial secondary metabolites (e.g., phenoloxidase products, reactive oxygen and nitrogen species) (Khan and Rolff, 2024; Zdybicka-Barabas et al., 2025). Metals, pesticides, and endocrine disrupters can affect these immune responses by decreasing the number of hemocytes, inhibiting the production of AMPs, and blocking signalling pathways involved in pathogen recognition (Khan and Rolff, 2024). Consequently, insects exposed to immune-suppressing contaminants may be more susceptible to infections and suffer increased mortality rates due to reduced fitness. Moreover, at the population level, this type of immune system suppression will likely facilitate pathogen transmission and increase mortality, leading to declines that can cascade to community and ecosystem processes, especially when faced with other anthropogenic pressures, such as habitat loss and climate change (Khan and Rolff, 2024).

Metabolic disruption: Metabolism disruptors interfere with the normal biochemistries employed by insects for energy balance, growth, and detoxification. Those compounds can interfere with essential enzymes and metabolic pathways, breaking nutrients into their elemental constituents or detoxifying deleterious substances. For instance, the chemicals that may block P450 enzymes could decrease activity within an organ or even reduce the ability to process the contaminant, resulting in bioaccumulation, leading directly to toxicity. Metabolic disturbances could also disrupt energy production via the deteriorated function of mitochondria, which reduces adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis required for cellular activities. Metabolic disruption in insects has widespread consequences for insect growth and reproduction, or can lead to greater susceptibility to the environment. Furthermore, metabolic disruptors can inhibit the formation of key biomolecules (e.g., lipids and proteins), resulting in disrupted growth during development or death (Li et al., 2024).
Assessment endpoints

The severity and type of toxic effect can be evaluated by measuring specific endpoints that may relate to the chemical’s expected mode of action and determining how contaminants impact insects in acute or chronic exposure scenarios or both. The most general classification of the responses, or the assessment endpoints, would be the lethal and sublethal ones. Despite a high range of endpoints used in ecotoxicology, in this section, the assessment endpoints are grouped based on organism response levels like those previously presented by González-Alcaraz et al. (2020), as follows: (1) bioaccumulation, (2) sub-organism level responses (such as genotoxicity and biochemical alterations), and (3) individual and population-level responses (life-history traits – lethality, reproduction, development, and behaviour). Each assessment endpoint may consist of several measured endpoints, as summarised in Table 1. Whenever available, examples from the edible insect ecotoxicity studies were provided. Otherwise, useful references are provided for other non-edible insect species besides bees (Table 1).

Bioaccumulation: Bioaccumulation refers to the process by which an organism uptake and retains contaminants from its environment at rates faster than it can eliminate them. This process occurs through various routes, as mentioned above. Over time, contaminants can accumulate within tissues, reaching concentrations that may harm the organism or proceed further into higher levels of the food chain. Contaminants such as metals, pesticides, and persistent organic pollutants (POPs) are of particular concern, as they can bioaccumulate and potentially biomagnify through trophic levels, leading to widespread ecological and health risks.

The most common endpoint related to the bioaccumulation of contaminants in edible insects is the measured concentration of the contaminant in the organism (e.g., Gulsunoglu et al., 2019; Jankauskienė et al., 2024; Truzzi et al., 2019, 2020). The parameter often calculated when this endpoint is measured is the bioaccumulation factor – BAF (Truzzi et al., 2020, 2022) or biota-substrate accumulation factor – BSAF (Cardoso et al., 2023, 2024), presenting the ratio between the concentration of the contaminant and the concentration in the feed. Toxicokinetics modelling applied to the experimental datasets can be a valuable tool to not only discriminate if the edible insects eliminate the contaminant, but also to infer the efficiency of the elimination and/or the critical time necessary for the complete elimination. One of the endpoints used is BAFkinetics, calculated by the ratio between the uptake (K1) and elimination rate constant (K2) (Cardoso et al., 2023, 2024). The section Data analysis and interpretation in this article describes the basics of toxicokinetics.

Sub-organismal level responses:

(1) Genotoxicity: Some environmental contaminants can reach the nucleus of cells and interact directly with DNA, causing DNA damage. Different techniques have been used to assess DNA damage; one of the most used is the comet assay, considered a simple, quick, and inexpensive method. In insects, the first report using the comet assay was for the fruit fly D. melanogaster (Bilbao et al., 2002). The comet assay has been used in different insect species (Augustyniak et al., 2016), providing important information on understanding cellular response to genotoxic compounds. Although the genotoxicity of environmental pollutants in insects has been explored, those studies are still scarce for a specific group of insects, such as edible insects (Kheirallah et al., 2019). Another interesting point to consider is the efficiency of DNA repair. Insect populations differ regarding their ability to repair DNA damage, depending on their lifespan, stage of life, habitat/environment, and evolutionary history (Azqueta et al., 2014). However, little is known about these mechanisms in insects. This is highly relevant considering the continuous exposure to environmental genotoxic compounds, sometimes over multiple generations of organisms. These studies can provide critical insights into the mechanisms of genotoxicity and help establish safe thresholds for contaminants in insect farming. Linking genotoxicity data to bioaccumulation studies also aids in assessing whether insects metabolise or retain genotoxic substances, enabling the development of regulatory guidelines for insect-based food and feed products.

(2) Biochemical endpoints: Biochemical endpoints serve as early warning indicators of exposure to contaminants in edible insects and provide insights into their physiological responses to stressors. These endpoints involve measurable biochemical changes at the molecular or cellular level, such as alterations in enzyme activities, antioxidant defence mechanisms, or stress protein expression. For edible insects reared on substrates that may contain contaminants, biochemical endpoints are invaluable for assessing their health, resilience, and safety as food or feed. Biomarkers are molecular or biochemical measures that help identify exposure to contaminants on the cellular level. These endpoints may involve alterations in gene expression, the activity of a particular enzyme, or stress-susceptible proteins. When insects present elevated detoxification enzymes, like the cytochrome P450s or antioxidant markers (e.g., SOD), they are responding to chemical stress, possibly through the activation of heat shock proteins (HSPs) against cellular damage and mal-adaptation (Nauen et al., 2022). Responses on the biochemical level can provide early warning signatures of exposure before overt signs of toxicity, such as changes in behaviour or reproduction, and are readily amenable to measurement. Biomarkers, meanwhile, also indicate the mechanism of toxicity and tell researchers how these compounds react at the molecular level. These biochemical changes provide insights into how insects cope with contaminants and have implications for their suitability as food or feed. For instance, the overexpression of stress-related proteins or excessive ROS production might affect the nutritional quality of insect-derived products. Integrating biochemical endpoint data with bioaccumulation studies can help identify thresholds of contaminant exposure that do not compromise insect health or consumer safety. Ultimately, biochemical endpoints contribute to refining rearing practices and ensuring safe and sustainable insect-based product production. However, some research is still needed to set baseline thresholds that provide insect fitness categories.

Individual and population-level responses: Individual and population-level responses are critical endpoints in evaluating the effects of contaminants on edible insects. These responses assess how exposure to toxicants impacts insect survival, reproduction, development, and behaviour, and how these effects influence population dynamics. Understanding these responses is crucial for ensuring the viability of insect farming systems and the safety of insect-based food and feed. At the individual level, lethal effects, such as mortality, are easily quantified. While mortality is a straightforward and commonly assessed endpoint, sub-lethal effects, such as impaired reproduction, delayed development, or abnormal behaviour, can provide more nuanced insights into long-term contaminant impacts. The endocrine-disrupting herbicide atrazine offers one example of chemical interference with sexual selection in the mealworm beetle T. molitor (McCallum et al., 2013). The simultaneous exposure of a triazine pesticide (metribuzin-based) and mineral fertiliser (nitrogen, potassium, and phosphorus-based) to T. molitor through a moistened filter paper caused different accumulation patterns among newly emerged adult males and females for the metals and rare earth elements originating from those products. The study raises the question of potential differences in energy allocation for reproduction and immune responses among the sexes and further consequences on reproductive success and the population (Naccarato et al., 2023).

To further explore these energy allocation dynamics, tools like the Dynamic Energy Budget (DEB) model provide a comprehensive framework for understanding how contaminants affect individual physiology, helping to extrapolate these effects to population-level outcomes (Jager et al., 2014). It quantifies energy flows within organisms, enabling the evaluation of physiological stress responses, growth trajectories, and reproductive potential. For instance, the effects of a polystyrene diet on T. molitor larvae were explored using the DEB model. This study revealed that polystyrene-fed larvae experienced significant growth, development, and survival reductions due to insufficient reserves and inadequate energy allocation. These effects highlight the inability of polystyrene to sustain life processes, failing to complete the life cycle in this edible species (Matyja et al., 2020). These findings underline the importance of understanding energy dynamics under environmental stressors and their implications for edible insect species’ reproductive success and population sustainability. At the population level, these individual-level effects can cascade into broader impacts, compromising population stability and growth. Furthermore, this study illustrates how modelling approaches such as DEB can quantify physiological stress responses and project their consequences on population viability, advancing our ability to assess and mitigate risks in insect farming systems (Maino and Kearney, 2015).

(1) Lethality: Lethality, or mortality, is the toxicity trial’s most straightforward and common outcome. It assists in finding the lethal concentration (LC50) or a lethal dose (LD50) that induces mortality to 50% of the test population. Although lethality is a crucial endpoint when assessing compound toxicity, it offers no insight into sublethal effects that could alter population dynamics in the long term. Toxicologists use mortality as a straightforward and quantitative outcome, but in a more realistic scenario, chronic impacts are certainly more relevant, deriving behavioural impairments or reproductive effects. As such, while overall mortality is an essential endpoint of toxicity testing, it should be complemented with additional sublethal metrics to holistically evaluate the impact of exposures on edible insects for mechanistic understanding of hazard assessment. Insect’s knockdown is a proxy for mortality, measured as the rate at which an animal is no longer moving but is still breathing. This endpoint is especially noticeable for flying insects.

(2) Reproduction: Reproduction-related endpoints include the number of eggs laid, hatching rate, and offspring survival. Toxins can interfere with hormonal regulation, inhibit reproduction and/or lower reproductive rate. For example, endocrine-disrupting chemicals such as organochlorines might disrupt egg production in female butterflies or cause developmental anomalies among their offspring (Kumar and Holt, 2014). Characterising reproductive success can involve evaluating multigenerational consequences, as exposure in one generation may produce effects that persist to subsequent generations (with reportable impacts on fertility or developmental timelines). Conducting sensitivity assays on the F1 generation (offspring) provides a more comprehensive insight into the long-term effects of contaminant exposure and evaluation of insect populations for persistence in contaminated environments (Kafel et al., 2014).

(3) Development: Developmental endpoints are related to the effects of contaminants on life stages in insects, namely on moulting, metamorphosis, and growth. Delays in development, deformities, or failures to moult or metamorphose are strong indicators of endocrine disruption or other toxic effects (Noriega and Nouzova, 2020). The juvenile hormone analogues keep insects from moulting through their metamorphic stages, often leading to misshapen adults or failing to achieve reproductive maturity (Parthasarathy and Palli, 2021). Population declines can occur due to these developmental abnormalities, with affected individuals dying before reproducing (or producing non-viable offspring) and growing to adulthood, but unable to reproduce or pass the mutation to future generations (vanEngelsdorp et al., 2009).

(4) Behaviour: Various changes in behaviour can be indicators of insect exposure to contaminants. Such modifications can range from disordered locomotion and reduced feeding activity with induced avoidance behaviours to alterations in mating activities and communication pathways. This may result in paralysis or the loss of coordinated movements, which makes it difficult for predators to find them and reduces accessibility to food. This could be easily explained by giving an example of pesticide exposure that induces neurotoxicity; these toxicants can impair the foraging behaviour of insects, decreasing the mating success and causing a reduced reproductive output, with consequences for the population structure (Richardson et al., 2019). Furthermore, avoiding contaminated environments might change the ecological functions of certain insects, such as pollination or prey-predator relations. Look at these behavioural changes is essential since many chemicals are toxic at doses lower than the lethal dose, impacting other behavioural parameters, such as physiological delays or changes in species interactions.
3 Methodologies

The following section will approach and propose different methodologies in insect ecotoxicology research. These methods range from broad exposure tests, where insects are exposed to contaminated media in their environment, to more targeted approaches, which involve localised exposure of specific body parts to a controlled source of contamination. The selection of each test or method highly depends on the study’s specific objectives. Some are designed to provide a broad overview of toxic effects across the whole organism, while others are more specific and targeted, focusing on particular tissues or systems. Furthermore, the endpoints to be assessed, such as mortality, physiological alterations, or biochemical changes, will depend on the type of exposure chosen, ensuring that the methodology aligns with the objective of the investigation.

One of the broader exposure methods uses a spray containing a solution of a chemical of interest that disperses the solution into a specific exposure area, typically around 9 cm in diameter. The Potter spray tower (Figure 2) is internationally recognised as the reference technique for chemical spraying in insects, applying a fine mist of chemical solutions of interest (Roychoudhury et al., 2016). This technique provides a uniform and controlled exposure, which is highly valuable for laboratory-based toxicology assays. This method is particularly useful in insecticide testing, as it allows precise and reproducible applications of chemicals across the entire surface area of the organism, simulating environmental exposure scenarios where insects encounter contaminants as a proxy for pesticide spraying in agriculture. From the edible insects’ perspective, these tests can provide valuable information regarding the potential effects and/or bioaccumulation of different pesticides in crop fields, particularly in areas where wild insect harvesting is still a fact. It is a typical small-scale laboratory bioassay and is frequently used for testing the effects of insecticides, fungicides, or other chemical compounds on small insect species like Hermetia illucens, Tenebrio molitor or Acheta domesticus.

The mist can be applied directly to the insects (Figure 2a) or surfaces (Figure 2b), such as plants or glass plates, with which the insects come into contact on a subsequent exposure. The precision of the Potter spray tower allows for the control and establishment of the concentration of the chemical, the droplet size, and the coverage. This technique also allows different exposure times and durations that can be controlled and decided upon, considering the type of contaminant to be tested. While the Potter spray tower offers precise and reproducible results, some limitations could be pointed out. It is unsuitable for large-scale applications, as the apparatus is designed for small surface areas and laboratory settings. Additionally, the method assumes that the mist evenly coats the entire insect body, which may not always be environmentally relevant, as factors such as wind or soil may affect exposure patterns in the field.

Herron et al. (2014) assessed the aphid (Aphis gossypii) susceptibility to the insecticides phorate and sulfoxaflor. The organisms were transferred to leaf discs and were then sprayed with serial concentrations of the insecticides through a Potter spray tower. After 24 hours, mortality was assessed. Herron et al. (1998) tested different species, including scales, aphids, thrips, mites, and mealybugs, on petroleum-based spray oils. The petroleum spray oils were diluted and sprayed onto test subjects using the Potter spray tower. Mortality was assessed after 24 hours for mites and aphids and up to 27 days for scales. To our knowledge, no studies have directly evaluated the effects of this procedure on edible insect species.
Dipping method

This method involves submerging insects (Figure 3a) or plant materials (Figure 3b) directly into a chemical solution for a short period (seconds). It is typically used when other exposure methods, such as spraying or topical application, are impractical. It is widely used in laboratory studies to assess the effects of contaminants on small or fragile insects that cannot easily be sprayed or handled in large numbers.

The exposure time is carefully controlled, typically lasting just a few seconds, to ensure the physical action of dipping does not harm the organism. Afterwards, the insects can dry, and their behaviour, survival, or physiological responses are monitored over time. This method can also involve dipping plant materials (e.g., leaves) into the chemical solution and using it to feed herbivorous insects, allowing oral exposure, or using it as a substrate, allowing the insects to absorb the chemicals through the cuticle.

The dipping method is applied to small, plant-feeding insects such as aphids, whiteflies, or thrips and fragile insect life stages like eggs or larvae. It is also widely used for stored-product pests and housefly larvae. Researchers may use this method when precision or controlled exposure is required, such as when testing insecticide efficacy on delicate insect stages or when other methods are too invasive. One of the main limitations of the dipping method is the risk of harming or drowning the insects during dipping, mainly if the timing or technique is not carefully controlled. Likewise, this methodology can be stressful to the organism if it is not previously acclimated to lower temperatures to slow its metabolism, and there is a risk of over- or under-exposure to a contaminant, as controlling the exposure time can be difficult. Moreover, this method does not reflect natural exposure scenarios, where insects are rarely fully immersed in chemicals in their natural environment.

For the dipping technique, organisms can be picked up with forceps and dipped directly into an aqueous chemical solution for a short period, either a suspension or an emulsion (Story et al., 2021). Although not used with insects, Zhu et al. 2018 applied the dipping method by dipping two-day-old spider hatchlings into various solutions containing the insecticides triflumezopyrim, thiamethoxam, chlorpyrifos, and abamectin. The dipping was performed for 20 seconds; afterwards, spiders were placed on absorbent paper to remove excess solution. Individual organisms were then placed into glass petri dishes, and mortality was assessed at 2, 4, and 6 days after exposure (Zhu et al., 2018). Fresh mulberry leaves with lustre were selected, weighed, and sprayed with water using leaf-dipping. The leaves were dried and dipped in the liquid pesticides (imidacloprid and thiamethoxam) for 10 seconds. After air-drying, leaves were placed into culture dishes and fed to second-instar silkworms of the species Bombyx mori (Chen et al., 2023).
Fumigation method

The fumigation method exposes insects to airborne contaminants in a sealed environment, typically simulating conditions encountered in stored-product facilities or enclosed spaces. It is commonly used in studies focusing on the effects of volatile chemicals, such as fumigants, on insect pests. This method is particularly effective for studying pests that infest stored goods, such as beetles, moths, and mites.

In this method, insects are placed in a closed chamber, and a gaseous contaminant is introduced (Figure 4). The concentration of the gas, as well as the temperature and humidity inside the chamber are carefully controlled. The insects are exposed to the gas for a specific duration, returning them to their culture medium or a recovery room to assess later endpoints, such as insect mortality rates, physiological responses, or behavioural changes over time.

This method can be adequate for testing applications in rearing facilities when fungi or other pests that harm insects occur. On the other hand, it presents some limitations as it is primarily suited for controlled, enclosed environments and may not accurately reflect field conditions where gas concentrations fluctuate, or where insects can access fresh air. Additionally, some insect species may develop resistance to fumigants, requiring repeated exposure or higher concentrations to achieve the desired effect. The method is also limited to gaseous contaminants and may not provide information on other routes of exposure, such as ingestion or contact.

The fumigation toxicity of three benzoate compounds, methyl benzoate, vinyl benzoate, and ethyl benzoate, was evaluated against phosphine-susceptible and phosphine-resistant strains of the lesser grain borer (Rhyzopertha dominica) and rice weevil (Sitophilus oryzae). The experiments were conducted in glass fumigation chambers where mixed-sex adult insects were exposed to various concentrations of the benzoates for 24 hours. Exposure was done by applying the tested benzoate to the inner surface of a piece of parafilm and then affixing it to the underside of the chamber lid (Mostafiz et al., 2024).
Ingestion tests

Ingestion tests involve feeding insects with contaminated food or water to assess the toxicity/bioaccumulation of chemicals through oral exposure (Figure 5). This method is commonly used in studies that evaluate the ecological impacts of contaminants on insect populations, as it simulates how insects might encounter contaminants in their natural environment, or when provided in rearing facilities (e.g., different growing substrates).

Among the standardised tests, an example is the ISO 20963 (2005) developed to depict the lethal effects of pollutants on the insect larvae Oxythyrea funesta, which grows on soils and feeds on roots. In the ISO, O. funesta is exposed to contaminants by cuticular and alimentary uptake, evaluating survival rates.

From a food safety perspective, more recently, these assays have been conducted to assess the different edible species’ bioaccumulative capacity, providing information on how insects could accumulate contaminants from their rearing substrates. We can derive bioaccumulation factors and uptake and elimination constant rates depending on the study’s objectives. Alongside the accumulation patterns in edible insects, the elimination capacity of insects has also been a topic of concern. In toxicokinetic approaches, after a determined exposure period (e.g., 5 days for H. illucens, 21 days for T. molitor, and 10 days for A. domesticus), an equal period in a clean substrate (non-contaminated) is enough to confirm if and how insects eliminate the contaminants.

One limitation of this method is that insects may refuse to eat contaminated food, leading to a certain variability in exposure. Additionally, some species may avoid certain types of food, making it challenging to ensure consistent ingestion across all test subjects. The method is also limited to ingestion-based exposure and does not account for other routes, such as contact or inhalation. Furthermore, this will depend on the feeding preferences of the insect and can be challenging but adaptable, considering their feeding strategy.

Cardoso et al. (2023, 2024) used diet exposure to study the effects of contaminated food on representative edible species, demonstrating its utility in evaluating the safety of insects as food and feed sources. In both cases, the authors understand how insects accumulate metals (mercury – Hg) and Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs; Benzo[a]pyrene – BaP) from the substrates. However, they likely eliminate them very fast, proposing a depuration period after exposure to not-so-well-characterised substrates. The substrates are spiked with the test chemicals in laboratory conditions, adapting the procedure to the contaminant’s chemical properties, including adequate solvents (Cardoso et al., 2023, 2024). The abovementioned leaf-dipping method can be used as part of oral exposure (Chen et al., 2023)
Topical application method

This application involves directly applying a chemical contaminant to a specific part of the insect’s body, allowing researchers to observe localised effects such as cuticle penetration, irritation, or toxicity (Figure 6). This method is widely used in insecticide studies with insects as target species, as it allows for precise control over the dosage and location of exposure. However, the method may not reflect natural exposure scenarios, where insects simultaneously encounter contaminants through multiple routes. Usually, anaesthesia (typically with a short exposure to ice or CO2) is used to reduce their metabolism and facilitate the application of the test substance, affecting the insects’ physiological responses, especially in species sensitive to handling or low temperatures. It is often limited to short-term, acute toxicity studies and may not provide insight into chronic or sub-lethal effects.

Test organisms are selected and acclimatised under controlled conditions. The application uses a syringe or moistened swab, and a small amount of contaminant is applied directly to a designated body part. It can be applied to legs, antennae, or even the dorsal side, thorax, or abdomen, and applied uniformly using a solvent if necessary. Insects are observed for signs of toxicity like lethargy, impaired movement, or behavioural changes over time, and mortality, with regular checks during the first 24–72 hours. Being a highly beneficial method for studying the localised effects of insecticides or other contaminants on specific tissues or organs, it is commonly employed in short-term toxicity studies to determine lethal doses (LD50). The topical application applies to various insect species, regardless of size, and could be applied to most edible insect species. The protocol was developed and tested for mosquitoes (unspecified species) and fruit flies (Drosophila melanogaster). Yet, it can be easily adapted for other species and is characterised by a short exposure period. After exposure, fruit flies were knocked down, and mortality rates were assessed after 24–72 hours, depending on the mortality rates (Jensen et al., 2022). In another study with the mosquito Anopheles spp., the test duration was 1 and 24 hours (Norris and Coats, 2022).

The injection method introduces a contaminant directly into the insect’s body using a fine needle, providing precise control over the volume and location of exposure (Figure 7). This method is often used in toxicological studies where the direct introduction of a substance into the hemocoel is required to assess systemic effects. A hypodermic needle, very thin stainless steel (e.g., 27 or 30 gauge thickness and 0.41 or 0.30 mm in diameter), injects a small volume (usually 10 μl) of the contaminant into the insect’s body, typically into the hemocoel (body cavity) (Bressan et al., 2006). The contaminant should be applied through a saline solution whenever possible. This approach ensures the medium is compatible with the organism’s internal environment, minimising osmotic stress and ion imbalance. The negative control should follow the same methodology for injection, solely injecting organisms with the saline solution without the tested contaminant.

Depending on the study’s objectives, injections can be administered to specific body parts, such as the thorax or abdomen. Special care is taken to avoid causing excessive damage or bleeding, which could affect the results. The injected contaminant is rapidly distributed throughout the insect’s circulatory system, allowing researchers to study its effects on a systemic level. This method allows for assessing the exact amount of toxicant inside the insect’s body that causes the effects.

Injection is commonly used for larger insects (greater than 3 mm) where precision is possible. It is ideal for studies that require accurate dosage control and localised effects within the body. The method is often employed in research on the toxicokinetics of contaminants, assessing the direct impact of insecticides and avoiding or excluding the chance of metabolising the active compound through absorption in the organism’s gut. The latter was carried out in the study of the cytotoxicity of oxidised phytosterols by injection into the larvae of Tenebrio molitor (Meyer et al., 1998). However, injection is invasive and can cause harm to the insect, requiring a skilled technician to perform the procedure. Small or fragile insects may be difficult to inject without causing significant damage. Moreover, injection bypasses natural barriers (such as the cuticle) and may not reflect real-world exposure scenarios where contaminants enter the body through ingestion or contact. The method is also time-consuming, as each insect must be handled individually. However, there are available well-defined protocols to inject small insects (<3 mm), such as an improved nano-volume injection technique using a modified vacuum stage to administer a 10-nL solution of nuclease-free mineral oil in either the thoracic or abdominal areas of organisms of the species Frankliniella occidentalis (Raffin et al., 2022). This study also demonstrated the difference in organism survival between injections in different body areas, as the abdomen-injected group showed a significantly lower survival rate when compared to both the control and the thorax-injected group.
4 Data analysis, modelling, interpretation, and case studies
Data analysis and interpretation

The primary purpose of ecotoxicology experiments is to acquire knowledge on the concentration-response relationships of any chemical, material, or mixture so that it can be used in multiple contexts:

    (1) To improve our understanding of routes, mechanisms, and consequences, and the factors that mediate or confound the response to pollutants.
    (2) To define clear regulation thresholds of acceptable adverse effects that any compound, material, or mixture should meet before widespread use or application.
    (3) To contribute with instruments that aid regulation by assessing the risk associated with any compound, material, or mixture or, on the contrary, to perform a diagnostic assessment that allows monitoring the status of environmental compartments.
    (4) To contribute to developing improved, safer, and cleaner products, following sustainability concepts such as “safe and sustainable by design”.

These multiple purposes underlie the need to adapt all supporting methodologies, from the experimental design to the data analysis. An a priori definition is thus essential since it has downstream implications for the entire process.

Ecotoxicity data must meet high-quality standards, ensuring biological relevance, statistical robustness, control of confounding factors, replicability, and transparency in reporting. In this context, standardisation has been essential for defining the criteria for execution, quality, and control, facilitating the logistics of high-throughput testing, ensuring comparability and replicability, and clearly defining the endpoints, their measurement timings, and the statistical data treatment and reporting (Landis et al., 2017). Validation criteria must be clearly set as they are crucial for accepting data for regulatory purposes. Therefore, careful adoption of these tools is advised whenever possible. However, some guidelines may be less efficient for specific research questions and must fully comply with the latest statistical soundness, objectivity, and transparency paradigms (Keddig et al., 2015; Landis et al., 2017).

Considering all possible sources of bias and error before designing the experiment is essential. Power analysis is a crucial tool in ensuring that the quality of concentration-response relationships is compatible with the specific objectives of the experiment, the logistical constraints, and the ethical issues involved. Defining the experimental design consists of optimising the number of replicates, treatments, and subjects per replicate. It should be based on prior knowledge (e.g., historical data) regarding the intrinsic variability of the organism and endpoint measured, the biological relevance of the effects, and the statistical analysis to be implemented (Green et al., 2018).

Two main approaches are routinely used in ecotoxicology for analysing concentration-response relationships: (i) hypothesis testing and (ii) curve-fitting or regression modelling (Green et al., 2018).

These approaches directly relate to the main concepts/terminologies used to describe the toxicity effects: (i) NOEC/LOEC and related concepts, determined via hypothesis testing; (ii) LCx/ECx/ICx and others, estimated by regression. Both concepts are particularly relevant for regulation since they constitute the backbone of risk assessment and management decisions.

Hypothesis testing: These methods aim to test whether adverse effects are related to the test chemical. The precise formulation of null and alternative hypotheses is thus the first step within a comprehensive statistical analysis workflow. The type of statistical analysis depends on the experimental design, the nature of the data collected, and whether model assumptions are met, among others. Strictly monotone data are typically analysed with so-called “trend tests”, whereas “pairwise tests” can be employed for hypothesis testing of non-monotone data (Green et al., 2018). For both strategies, the main goal is to determine toxicity threshold values, such as the No Observed Effect Concentration (NOEC, highest concentration not statistically different from the control) or the Lowest Observed Effect Concentration (LOEC, lowest concentration statistically different from the control).

Despite the recent criticism of NOEC/LOEC values, their relevance within chemical regulation processes remains intact, and their use continues to be widespread (Landis et al., 2017). A thoughtful analysis of the merits and drawbacks of NOEC/LOEC is beyond the scope of this article. This approach has, however, substantial implications for the definition of the experimental design since the efficiency of NOEC/LOEC identification is greatly affected by sample size. Whenever NOEC/LOEC is the target toxicity descriptor, enough replication should be ensured to overcome the intrinsic biological variability of the endpoint.

Most data collected from ecotoxicity experiments is continuous (e.g., length, weight, enzyme activity, bioaccumulation, etc.) or can be transformed into a continuous format and analysed similarly (e.g., mortality, reproduction, etc., which can be converted to per cent of the control value). Analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by appropriate multiple comparison tests, is, by far, the most widely used statistical approach for hypothesis testing in the ecotoxicology of rearing insects (Alam et al., 2020; Janković-Tomanić et al., 2023; Khodaparast et al., 2021). However, ANOVA models imply strong assumptions concerning how the data must conform to the model, such as the normality of residuals and homogeneity of variances. These assumptions can be tested with appropriate statistical tools or visual inspection of residual patterns, and alternatives should be attempted whenever ANOVA requirements are not met. Model assumptions define how the data must conform to a chosen framework. However, in practice, model selection should always be guided by the characteristics of the data to ensure robust and biologically meaningful inference. Common options include data transformation with appropriate normalising or variance-stabilising functions (e.g., log, square root) or embracing non-parametric statistical procedures, whose assumptions are less demanding (e.g., Mann–Whitney, Kruskall–Wallis). Both alternatives are helpful but not without shortcomings, which should be considered when interpreting the results. Whereas the issues arising from data transformation have long been subject to debate (Ives, 2015; O’Hara and Kotze, 2010; Warton and Hui, 2011), non-parametric tests usually imply a compromise in statistical power, increasing the risk of non-rejection of a correct null hypothesis (Szöcs and Schäfer, 2015). Pairwise tests should be used for non-monotone datasets to avoid the determination of nonsensical NOECs or LOECs. As for trend tests, parametric and non-parametric options are also available (Green et al., 2018). An important issue regarding pairwise tests is to account for the number of comparisons to control, which can inflate the rate of false positive results. This can be done by controlling the family-wise error rate with multiplicity adjustment approaches, such as the Bonferroni correction or others.

Generalised linear models (GLMs) have recently been advocated as an alternative framework for analysing ecotoxicology datasets due to their higher flexibility, which provides a more appropriate option than transforming the data, since they allow the model to follow the data distribution rather than forcing the data into a restrictive framework (Szöcs and Schäfer, 2015). Frequently, the data collected from ecotoxicology experiments are not continuous but discrete (e.g., counts – reproductive output/number of offspring produced, etc.) or binomial (e.g., proportions – survival, malformations, etc.). GLMs enable the retention of the non-normal data configuration, avoiding bias and loss of information caused by data transformation, minimising distribution-related issues with model assumptions, and thus offering a natural solution for non-normal datasets (Szöcs and Schäfer, 2015). In addition, GLMs (i) often outperform classical methods for such data types in terms of statistical power and (ii) can be practically upgraded to handle more complex data structures, such as non-independent measurements, by including random effects (GLMMs, Generalised linear mixed models). Quantal responses and counts can be analysed using GLMs with binomial and Poisson error structures, respectively, if no signs of overdispersion are detected (e.g., higher variance than the mean). If overdispersion proves to be an issue, beta-binomial and negative-binomial error structures can be an option for quantal data and counts (Green et al., 2018). There is a plethora of theoretical resources, implementation routines, and model performance techniques focused on GLM, but it is beyond the scope of the current article.

Regression modelling: Regression modelling, or curve fitting, differs fundamentally from hypothesis testing and has often been mentioned as a better alternative to describe toxicity responses (Landis et al., 2017). Whereas the latter seeks to find thresholds of significant differences inherently determined by the experimental design, regression analysis relies on a parametric description of concentration-response curves, directly linking concentrations to specific biological effect levels (Keddig et al., 2015). This distinction has obvious implications for the definition of experimental design since the emphasis should no longer be on replication, but on an appropriate coverage of the entire concentration-response curve. Increasing replication can help decrease the confidence intervals (CI), but has fewer implications for estimating specific effective concentrations. However, the possibility of calculating CI across the entire concentration-response curve is an advantage of regression methods and should be considered when designing regression-oriented experiments (Landis et al., 2017).

The concentrations denoting x% biological effect are referred to as effect concentrations (ECx) or lethal concentrations (LCx, lethality is being assessed). The half-maximal effective concentrations (EC50/LC50) are the most widely used in scientific literature and regulation, but different sizes of effects (EC5/LC5, EC10/LC10, EC20/LC20) can be targeted for increased realism. The quality of ECx estimates is, however, highly dependent on the quality of the data, the appropriateness of the model selected, and the quality of the model fit. Any attempt to estimate an ECx with unsoundly supported data or to conduct any extrapolation step beyond the range of data implies significant assumptions, can lead to uncertain estimates, and should be avoided in practice.

Selecting an appropriate model to describe the concentration-response curve is essential in the regression process. The most used models for continuous data are log-logistic, hockey-stick, hormetic, and Hill models, each with specific assumptions about curve configuration, multiple variants with different data demands, and general assumptions regarding normality and variance homogeneity (Green et al., 2018). Quantal data and counts can be transformed into continuous data and similarly analysed if assumptions are met, or can be modelled using specific non-normal models. For quantal data, common approaches include the Probit or Logit models, and for counts, Poisson regression or other models. A thorough revision of the available models for each context is beyond the scope of the article. Several tools are available to help users decide if a model is suitable for a particular data set (e.g., goodness-of-fit) or to compare multiple models and decide which is the best option (e.g., model selection; Akaike Information Criteria (AIC) compares models based on principles of parsimony (Green et al., 2018).

With ecotoxicology and risk assessment procedures increasingly focused on low-dose effects estimation and interpolation, the concept of Benchmark Dose (BMD) emerged as an a priori defined threshold eliciting an adverse biological response deemed to imply acceptable risk (e.g., BMR, for instance, 10% effect) (Hothorn, 2014; Jensen et al., 2019). BMD was developed as a robust alternative to NOECs, which uses the experimental concentration-response curves and is supported by sound uncertainty estimates (Hothorn, 2014). Whenever multiple concentration-response models are available, model selection or model averaging has been proposed to reduce the dependence of BMD on the shape of the model (Hothorn, 2014).

Time-to-event models: A criticism often addressed to toxicity thresholds, such as ECx or NOEC, is the inherent time-fixed value, ignoring that toxicity is a function of exposure concentration and duration. Time-fixed values overlook processes such as depuration or recovery and often make inefficient use of the data collected during toxicity experiments, with implications for the statistical power of the analysis. When conducting experiments, additional data is usually recorded for intermediate time points (e.g., hourly, daily, weekly, etc.), which could be used to improve the understanding of the time course toxicity patterns. The resulting data is called time-to-event data because it documents the specific time (or, more often, time interval) of occurrence of each event (e.g., mortality, reproduction, behaviour syndrome, mutation, etc.), allowing for the cumulative follow-up of the toxicity. Multiple time-to-event options are available for research in ecotoxicology, varying in complexity and assumptions (e.g., non-parametric, semiparametric, and parametric), which provide a flexible framework to improve risk assessment procedures. Time-to-event approaches are expected to strengthen toxicity assessments with holometabolous insects, as most edible insects.

Toxicokinetics modelling: Because the previous statistical methods are inherently descriptive, ecotoxicologists have called for a better understanding of the processes triggered by contaminants that may underlie toxicity for organisms and lead to human and environmental hazards. Mechanistic or process-based models consist of mathematical expressions (e.g., Ordinary Differential Equations – ODEs) developed based on sound theoretical assumptions that attempt to explain the dynamic patterns within a “system” (Jager, 2023). As they focus on explaining processes rather than describing empirical data, mechanistic models are regarded as superior in extrapolating predictions toward untested scenarios and offer relevant applied insight that can be of value to managers and stakeholders. Toxicokinetic (TK) models consist of kinetic models developed to describe and explain the processes of chemical absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) in different model organisms (Moes et al., 2022). These can be single- or multicompartmental models and display various levels of complexity, but always assume the simplification of organisms to estimate a set of tractable toxicokinetic parameters used to infer the patterns of chemical fate and accumulation over time. The fluxes and rates in and out of each biological compartment describe the uptake-elimination kinetics and predict any xenobiotic’s bioaccumulative potential. Since the specific processes are directly being considered, whenever measured accumulation patterns are not adequately explained, one can attempt to refine TK models with more informative assumptions (e.g., growth dilution, biotransformation, stored fraction, multiphase elimination), directly learning how the “system” works throughout the process of model building. An appropriate model selection framework (e.g., AIC) can be used to compare different TK models and infer the plausibility of their assumptions. TK models with insects have been extensively adopted in trophic ecotoxicology research, but are seldom used in food and feed safety research with edible insects. Cardoso et al. (2023) showed how adopting TK modelling could contribute to defining strategies to improve food/feed safety in insect farming. Bioaccumulation in edible insects has been chiefly assessed using bioaccumulation factors (BAF) (Truzzi et al., 2020, 2022), steady state constants that assume the organism is in equilibrium with its surrounding phase (Moes et al., 2022). Although helpful at a screening stage, this approach involves strong assumptions about partitioning and does not provide information about elimination, storage, or biotransformation processes.
Case studies
Case study A: Safety and sustainability perspective

Knowledge of contaminant elimination for rearing process optimisation. Cardoso et al. (2023) applied a toxicokinetics approach to study mercury (Cardoso et al., 2023) and benzo[a]pyrene (Cardoso et al., 2024) bioaccumulation in edible insect larvae (Hermetia illucens and Tenebrio molitor) exposed to contaminated food. The exposure to an artificially contaminated diet (uptake phase) and depuration in a clean (non-contaminated) diet (elimination phase) were carried out alongside the adaptations to the time of exposure of each insect species. Toxicokinetics modelling principles used in ecotoxicology were applied, with the uptake and elimination rates as measured endpoints of bioaccumulation (see sections Assessment endpoints and Data analysis and interpretation in this article). The mechanistic understanding of contaminant accumulation dynamics is crucial for regulating novel feed and food placement on the market. At the same time, it can contribute to circularity and sustainability in waste management, emphasising the capability of insects as waste bioconverters. For the insect-rearing industry, this knowledge can be of direct practical use as the producers can apply the method of depuration on clean substrate for several days (depending on the type of contaminants), leading to improvements in the production and management practices and product safety before processing insects for commercialisation (Cardoso et al., 2023, 2024).
Case study B: Regulatory perspective

Mechanistic knowledge on biomarker responses for decision-making and regulatory improvements. Mycotoxins are one of the most frequently assessed contaminant groups in the context of accumulation in edible insects due to their adverse effects on human and animal health (genotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, carcinogenicity, etc.). Risk assessment of chemicals generally involves the aspects of exposure relevance and biological relevance. Jankovic-Tomanic et al. (2023) tested the effects of mycotoxin zearalenone on biomarkers of oxidative stress, locomotion activity, biomass, and survival in Tenebrio molitor at the exposure doses (e.g., concentrations in the feed) relevant to the feed legislation. They also assessed the concentration of zearalenone in the larvae upon exposure, contextualising it within the food safety regulations. With such an approach, the study covers both exposure and biological relevance. Additionally, the range of endpoints assessed (accumulation, range of sublethal endpoints, lethal endpoint) provides a robust set of data connecting the mechanistic knowledge obtained with physiological endpoints to the adverse outcome at the organismal level (Jankovic-Tomanic et al., 2023; 2024). The same authors also assessed the effects at higher doses (above the benchmarks in the current legislation) for a better understanding of the mode of action of this contaminant (Jankovic-Tomanic et al., 2023). In this way, researchers bring ecotoxicology knowledge close to the experts and risk assessors who can use it for evidence-based decision-making and, eventually, regulatory improvements and inclusion into adverse outcome pathways frameworks (AOPs). It is worth mentioning, however, that in the case of mycotoxin effects in edible insects and potential transfer via the food chain, research efforts are currently also focused on filling in the knowledge gap regarding the metabolites and their roles. This information can be crucial for the abovementioned regulations.
Case study C: Food chain perspective

Contribution of ecotoxicity tools with edible insects to the feed/food safety. Ecotoxicity studies on the contaminant transfer from edible insects to higher trophic levels are scarce. However, such an approach could have a significant role in the safety of the entire food chain as a link between the ecotoxicity of contaminants to the rearing insects and the toxicity profile of the novel feed/food. A laboratory study with zebrafish Danio rerio fed with larvae of Hermetia illucens represents a simulation of a transfer of contaminants in the food chain (Truzzi et al., 2022). Zebrafish is a commonly used vertebrate model in ecotoxicology and developmental biology. This work evaluates the potential transfer of metal(loid) from the insect feed to the insect larvae and finally to the fish by looking into the entire life cycle of the fish (larvae, juvenile, and adult stages). Direct contaminant measurement of Cadmium (Cd), Lead (Pb), Nickel (Ni), Arsenic (As), and Hg in the insect feed, insect larvae, and fish was carried out (as one of the measured endpoints in bioaccumulation; see section Bioaccumulation). The results demonstrated that it is safe to use insects as feed in aquaculture, yet underlined the need for a case-by-case assessment as the transfer of potentially toxic contaminants depends on the fish’s feed quality and life stage (Truzzi et al., 2022).
5 Concluding remarks

Despite the large body of ecotoxicology literature available with a range of insect species, including those used as edible, there is no consensus on the methodologies in ecotoxicity assays. This article illustrates some examples of well-established ecotoxicity assays. It provides a road map for using ecotoxicity procedures and endpoints at different levels of biological organisation (throughout the text and Table 1). Tenebrio molitor and Hermetia illucens are the most frequently used as model edible insects in contaminant accumulation studies, yet are not officially part of standards or technical guidelines (TG), such as those of ISO or OECD. Clearly, the adaptations of the various methodologies with non-edible insects that we presented in Section 3 to the edible insects need to be conducted regarding the species-specific ecological traits, qualities of the substrates used, and climatic conditions in the rearing facilities. For instance, the OECD TG “Determination of Developmental Toxicity to Dipteran Dung Flies (Scathophaga stercoraria L. Scathophagidae), Musca autumnalis De Geer (Muscidae) (OECD, 2008) suggests the data treatment and reporting of ECx and NOEC analogous to those used for terrestrial invertebrates, in compliance with the TG focused ecotoxicology data analysis (OECD, 2006). Nevertheless, one should bear in mind that, as discussed in the section Data analysis and interpretation, it is often necessary to contextualise and involve other data treatment and modelling approaches following the experimental designs and purpose of the experiment. Standardisation of ecotoxicity bioassays is a time-consuming but essential process that can benefit research and development, facilitate comparison across studies, provide rapid screening results, and further help establish more complex experimental designs.

In conclusion, the field of ecotoxicology for edible insects is at a crossroads where research, industry, and policy must converge. The lack of standardised methodologies for toxicity assessments remains a critical challenge, necessitating adaptations to address species-specific traits and environmental conditions. Future progress will depend on interdisciplinary collaborations and integrating emerging technologies, such as omics and in silico tools, to refine risk assessments and enhance our understanding of toxicity mechanisms. Moreover, harmonising global safety standards is essential for ensuring the sustainable and ethical expansion of the edible insect sector. By addressing these challenges, ecotoxicology research can safeguard public and environmental health and contribute to broader goals of biodiversity preservation, ecosystem sustainability, and global food security.
