10 BugBook: Advancing insect utilization for food and feed: standardizing processing methods, enhancing techno-functional properties, and refining feeding trial protocols 
Abstract

This study presents specific baseline protocols and actionable recommendations for processing and evaluating insect-derived products for food and feed applications, addressing current gaps in standardization and market integration. Processing methods and the utilization of insect-derived products are two critical aspects intrinsically linked to their market applicability for both food and feed purposes. Therefore, it is imperative to direct research efforts towards these domains, aiming to standardise procedures and products to support broader market adoption. Currently, thermal processing, mechanical fractionation, and enzymatic hydrolysis are among the most common technologies, but differences in temperature, process duration, and input material result in variable products, which might be more suitable for specific applications rather than other. These inconsistencies hamper process standardization as well as stakeholder and market confidence. To address this, the study proposes a set of core variables – including moisture content, processing temperature, and duration – that should be consistently reported in scientific publications to enhance reproducibility and facilitate quality benchmarking. Conversely, the evaluation of insect-derived products as feed ingredients parallels the inclusion of other raw materials into animal diets. Similarities include assessing their nutritional value, digestibility, and compatibility with existing feed formulations. However, the integration of insect-based feed ingredients also necessitates addressing several unique recommendations and considerations that are specific to insects and their derivatives. Notably, the study identifies the need for refined protein quantification techniques (e.g. adjusted N conversion factors) that account for chitin and other non-protein nitrogen sources. Furthermore, insect-derived products are inherently diverse, encompassing a wide range of forms, including dried powders, liquid extracts, whole (live) insects, and processed meals. Each of these product types presents distinct challenges and opportunities in terms of their handling, storage, and integration into feed systems. To guide future application, we outline tailored strategies for each product type, emphasizing the importance of interdisciplinary research spanning animal physiology, behavior, and nutrition. Overall, this work provides a structured framework to advance the standardization, comparability, and integration of insect-derived products across the food and feed sectors.

1 Background and Motivation

Introducing any novel food or feed ingredient, particularly one as multifaceted as insects, requires a comprehensive and coordinated assessment of its nutritional, technological, and perceptual dimensions. Several essential aspects must therefore be evaluated, including palatability, digestibility, nutrient bioavailability, presence of undesirable components and food safety. Understanding these factors is crucial to ensuring that such ingredients are both nutritionally beneficial and accepted by the person or animal in question.

With respect to food application, the main obstacle preventing the full exploitation of insects in the food sector appears to be related to the low consumer acceptability. Processing insects in an unrecognisable form and/or extracting nutrients to be integrated into traditional food has been shown as a critical step capable of increasing acceptability (Sogari et al., 2023). However, although processing is always applied with the aim of improving some positive aspects of the treated product, the application of inadequate processing parameters or an erroneous choice of technologies might lead to undesirable effects (Ojha et al., 2021). Additionally, a given processing procedure applied on different products and associated component matrices might lead to different outcomes, suggesting that continuous research should be carried out (Liceaga, 2021). Therefore, although several producers have developed proper processing pathways, the amplitude of available options requires continuous iterative evaluation, suggesting that full standardisation of insect processing for food or feed application is not pursuable at this time. An important aspect which should not be overlooked is the sufficiency of information and data to reproduce a certain processing method and compare results. In this sense, the word “standardization” should assume a new meaning, which goes well beyond the simple uniformity of methodologies, embracing the concept of creating a series of well-defined and reproducible data which can help food and feed operators in selecting the best technological conditions for obtaining a certain product.

However, while such considerations are applicable to both, food and feed, the feed sector appears to be more complicated than the food sector. Animal producers are indeed willing to incorporate insect-based ingredients in animal diets only if their inclusion is beneficial for the animal and/or the farmer. Additionally, insects’ inclusion in animal diets usually relies on minimal processing, which only aims to achieve uniform diets, able to best satisfy the animal requirements (Rossi et al., 2025). Overall, the use of insect meal, oil and live form in livestock diets began with minimal data on digestibility, as nutrient absorption and bioavailability of insect-based feed components were initially unexplored across common livestock and aquaculture species. In these early stages, insect products were often included in animal diets without extensive testing, focusing primarily on establishing their applicability. Recent advancements in insect nutrition research have provided more data on the nutrient profiles and digestibility of insect meals, enabling the creation of preliminary dietary guidelines (Bellezza Oddon et al., 2024; Crosbie et al., 2020; Mastoraki et al., 2024; Matin et al., 2021). This growth of knowledge has helped to optimise the formulation of insect-based diets for various species, facilitating their integration into feed. Despite these advances, the inherent variability in nutrient composition across insect species, life stages, and rearing as well as processing methods remains a challenge (Bellezza Oddon et al., 2024). Consequently, it is advisable to conduct preliminary analyses of the proximate composition and digestibility of the specific insect products being used in any new study. This ensures that the formulation process is adapted to the precise nutritional characteristics of the insect ingredient and guarantees that experimental diets meet the target species’ nutritional requirements while controlling for price competitiveness. Additionally, for studies involving live larvae as a feed ingredient, similar analytical principles can be applied, with particular focus on the insect’s dry matter content.

Therefore, the primary goal of this Bugbook chapter is to establish comprehensive baseline protocols for valorising insects in the food and feed sectors. In the first part, several processing technologies and their effect on the quality of the final product are described. Minimal requirements for allowing reproducibility and inter-species comparability of specific processing methods are therefore listed and fractionation technologies commonly applied on edible insects for obtaining insect-derived ingredients are illustrated. Methods for analysing physical and techno-functional properties of insect ingredients are described in the second part, while the third part focuses on describing feed experimental protocols applicable across various livestock and aquaculture species. Chemical aspects associated with the use of insects in the food and feed sectors are considered along the overall chapter, although no specific methodologies and correspondent results are mentioned as they are already part of other specific Bugbook chapters.

By providing this structured approach, the authors aim to facilitate a more cohesive and rigorous research process that can better assess the impact of food processing on insect-derived products, as well as of insect-derived feed products on animal growth, and overall productivity. The most pertinent literature on insect processing and its application in food and feed was thoroughly reviewed and several recommendations were formulated on published guidelines, further enriched with the authors’ expertise.

This framework is designed to support both emerging researchers entering the field and experienced scientists seeking a standardized methodology to achieve more consistent and comparable results.
2 Insect processing for food and feed production
Choice of processing technology and parameters

With the growing acceptance of insects as food and feed sources, various processing methods have been tested and applied. Currently, industrial processing technologies for edible insects include blanching, drying, milling and cooking. They have been applied to treat both whole insects and insect fractions. Other innovative technologies such as high-pressure processing and fermentation have also been explored for insects, although their application has not yet been documented at the industrial level (Liceaga, 2021; Ojha et al., 2021). The main benefit of these novel technologies is their ability to replace traditional invasive processes, without strongly affecting the quality of the product. However, the elevated costs, particularly those associated with specialized equipment, along with the advanced level of expertise required to manage these operations, may deter their adoption, especially in cost-sensitive industries like the feed sector. Regardless of the applied technology, it is important to consider that every process will have an impact on the quality of the final product. The choice of technologies, processing parameters or even processes sequence might therefore lead to different products with variable properties. Every insect species is typically characterised by unique initial properties suggesting that a process optimised for one species might not work for another species and/or the obtained results might be significantly different from the expected ones. Additionally, insect origin, including rearing substrate, developmental stage and rearing conditions may affect the processing results. Therefore, it appears clear that standardising processing operations is not an easy task, and it might not always make sense. Research aiming to test different processing methods, conditions and combinations/sequences of operations is indeed deeply needed. However, despite a pure standardisation in food and feed processing is not applicable, recommendations should aim to describe and apply clearly defined processing methodologies so that every operation can be fully understood and reproduced. One aspect that should always be addressed when producing insects for food or feed is adherence to specific legislative constraints. Specific legislations are applicable in different countries, with production, processing and commercialisation of insects and insect-based products being strictly regulated. Processing is considered a key-step for ensuring quality and safety of the product, with the microbial safety being often regarded as a key aspect to be ensured along the production line (FAO, 2020; Galecki et al., 2023). GMT+ certification scheme offers several recommendation concerning the production of animal feed, which also includes insect products. On the other hand, food production as well as feed production for food-producing animals should consider the HACCP recommendations which, although mandatory in EU, might not always be required in other countries. Several HACCP plans addressing different manufacturing processes as well as various end-products have been developed (Arevalo et al., 2023; Fraqueza and Patarata, 2017; Kooh et al., 2020) and their application should always be considered along the entire production line, aiming to deliver a safe product to the final consumer (Okyere, 2023). Origin of the insect should also be considered and name of the company providing the insect source should always be disclosed in any research work.

Adherence to strict recommendation should however not be always considered at research level. Possibility of testing different processing parameters/options is indeed required in order to promote a better understanding of the sector, promoting innovation.
From insects to insect fractions: overview of fractionation technologies

As part of an increasingly diversified exploration of insects as food and feed sources in recent years, fractionation and extraction techniques for refining insect ingredients to be incorporated in several final products are gaining importance. Although direct use of insects is usually preferred to fully exploit the sustainability advantages that insects provide (Nowakowski et al., 2022), their direct use as food, i.e. eating whole insects is often rejected by consumers (Olivadese and Dindo, 2023). On the other hand, use of whole insects in feed applications appears to face fewer obstacles and could therefore be more easily adopted. Despite this, when insects are intended as animal feed, combination of the insect component with other materials such as fish oil or other ingredients is often indicated, suggesting that certain processing such as nutrients extraction and fractionation might be useful.

Fractionation of insect components is carried out through dry or wet methodologies. While wet fractionation usually allows to obtain products with higher purity (≥50%), dry fractionation appears to be more widely applied in food or feed production. The latter allows to obtain components free from chemical reagents, which may be safer for consumers (Sweers et al., 2022), while ensuring more appropriate techno-functional properties for food and feed formulations. Additionally, dry fractionation is generally associated with lower energy requirements, being therefore more environmentally friendly than wet fractionation (Sweers et al., 2022). On the other hand, the lower purity obtained through dry fractionation reflects higher levels of undesirable compounds, including, for example, ash and chitin, which might negatively influence digestibility, flavour and technical functionality of the final product, posing further challenges on consumer acceptance (Wendin and Nyberg, 2021).

Current research on fractionation of insects for utilising specific components in food and feed is therefore oriented to the combination of sustainability, efficiency and safety of the obtained products. As novel fractionation techniques are subject to continuous improvement and validation, highlighting a particular standard method for insect components extraction and fractionation is not trivial. Therefore, the following section aims to describe a standard pathway for insect fractionation, offering basic orientations based on which further improvements to enhance efficiency, sustainability and safety could be initiated. Wet fractionation is a common technique used for separation of soluble proteins from insoluble fractions as insoluble proteins, ash, lipids and chitin (Mishyna et al., 2021). Wet fractionation can be performed in a few simple steps, as demonstrated by Ravi et al. (2021), in which black soldier fly (BSF) larvae were blanched (95 °C/5 min), crushed and separated by centrifugation (8000 rpm/5 min). Different fractions represented by a lipid-enriched fraction, a creamy fraction, an aqueous fraction and a solid fraction, were therefore obtained (Ravi et al., 2021). However, further operations such as addition of specific solvents, pH manipulation, temperature modification and agitation/homogenisation might be further applied for improving extraction yield and/or fraction purity.

For example, blanching and/or cooking, which are typically applied on harvested insects with the aim of reducing microbial load, inhibiting degrading enzymes or minimising lipid oxidation, might also favourite higher protein extraction and digestibility (Larouche et al., 2019; Leni et al., 2019). Application of high temperature might indeed lead to protein denaturation and therefore enhance the extraction yields (Manditsera et al., 2019; Ssepuuya et al., 2020). Grinding might instead improve the solvent penetration, resulting in better extraction of desired compounds (Ravi et al., 2020). Similarly, defatting might be applied as a process improving protein fractionation (Choi et al., 2017). Therefore, all of these pre-treatments may strongly affect protein extraction yield as well as protein quality suggesting that both, sequence of operations and operation parameters (specific methodology applied, temperature, time, substrate to solvent ratio, solvents concentration, adjuvant activities, etc.) should always be carefully reported to allow reproducibility and comparison.

In wet fractionation, protein extraction from insects is typically carried out through alkaline or acidic solubilisation followed by isoelectric precipitation (Mishyna et al., 2021). Differently from other more traditional food, insect proteins present the maximum solubility at alkaline pH (8–13) with pH 10 or higher being often recommended as the best value for protein solubilisation (Bußler et al., 2016b; Ndiritu et al., 2019). Reference pH for ensuring the highest protein solubility appears however to depend on insect species, pre-treatment applied, and solvent used for defatting (Bußler et al., 2016a; Rose et al., 2023), suggesting that specific values cannot be defined, and a case-by-case evaluation should be carried out. For the purpose of this work, the authors therefore recommend to always specify every step and condition occurred during the fractionation procedure. Indications related to insect species, physical form, pre-treatment, extraction solvent, solvent concentration, solid/liquid ratio, adjuvant process (homogenisation, stirring, ultrasound, hydrolyses), etc., should therefore be reported, ascribing particular importance to the variables (temperature, time, intensity of the treatments, volumes of solvents, pH, etc.) that might directly or indirectly affect the extraction results. Solubilised proteins are then precipitated by adjusting the pH of the solution to values close to their isoelectric point. Insect protein precipitation occurs at pH values between 4 and 5, with some small differences between different insect species (Ma et al., 2023). Interesting is, however, to consider that, depending on the precipitation pH, different proteins might be precipitated, resulting in final products with significantly different protein composition and therefore techno-functional properties (Mishyna et al., 2021). Indication of the specific pH used for protein precipitation is therefore required to ensure reproducibility and comparability of the results. Protein extraction can also be conducted by water or salt solutions. Salt-assisted extraction methods significantly enhanced protein quality from edible insects, achieving a protein purity up to 73%, increasing solubility (>60% at pH 7.4) and improving oil and water holding capacity (Pokorski et al., 2025).

Dry fractionation, on the other hand, refers to the process of separating different nutritional components by exploiting their specific physical properties. It displayed several advantages over the wet fractionation technology such as avoiding extra water and chemical reagents (Sweers et al., 2022). These conditions indirectly result in a lower environmental impact associated with dry fractionation compared to wet fractionation. Additionally, as no water is employed in the process, less energy is needed during the drying step (Lie-Piang et al., 2021), making the process even more attractive for researchers and the industry (Pulivarthi et al., 2023). Despite these advantages, dry fractionation has not yet been widely adopted in the food industry. To date, only two studies have explored the application of dry fractionation to edible insects (Purschke et al., 2018a; Sipponen et al., 2018), indicating that further research is necessary to establish a standardized methodology. In contrast, more extensive data are available on the dry fractionation of vegetable proteins (Schutyser and van der Goot, 2011), which are primarily used in animal nutrition. Consequently, similar techniques are often applied to insect fractionation as well.

Similar to wet fractionation, in dry fractionation, harvested insects are separated from the frass, inactivated, and subjected to specific pre-treatments such as blanching, defatting and drying. Blanching is often used for microbial and enzymatic inactivation, colour stabilisation and protein denaturation. Defatting can be performed using ethanol, petroleum ether, methanol, or n-hexane at different sample/solvent ratios or through mechanical press (Sindermann et al., 2021). Drying allows to obtain a product that is stable and suitable for dry fractionation, although it might be responsible for tissue collapse and browning reactions (Purschke et al., 2018a). All of these preliminary steps might have a strong impact on the fractionation results. Any operation should therefore be carefully documented and information concerning insect species and stage, material form, sequence of operations, time, temperature, solvent type, solvent concentration, solid/solvent ratio, initial and final status of the material (e.g. initial dry matter and final dry matter), used devices and adjuvant operations should always be specified.

After drying, milling is a crucial step as it allows biomass fragmentation into a flour characterised by fragments with different weight, density, size and electric charging properties, which might therefore be mechanically separated. Although milling can be directly performed in only one stage (after drying and defatting) at the research level it is usually carried out in two stages: a first rough grinding aiming to increase the particles surface area and allowing better defatting is indeed followed by a finer grinding to obtain the desired particle size and release cellular components (Gao et al., 2020). Different types of mills might therefore need to be used, including both, traditional (hammer or knife mills) and more advanced ones (classifier or cryogenic mills). Choice of the most suitable mill should consider several aspects, including material characteristics, quality of the final product and dimension of the final particles. Different grinders might indeed directly affect the efficiency of the following separation operations.

Particle separation is then carried out through either airflow (based on both size and density of the material), sieves (based on the size of the ground material) or electrostatic separators (based on the electric properties of the sample). However, regardless of the applied separation system, two fractions represented by a protein-enriched fraction and a chitin fraction are usually obtained (Sipponen et al., 2018).
3 Physio-chemical characteristics and techno-functional analysis of insect products

When insects or insect-derived ingredients are directly used as food or feed, the physical as well as techno-functional properties of the material need to be addressed. Physical properties include the characteristics that directly affect the appearance and the functionality of a certain food. They consist of physical (e.g. shape, size, weight, colour, density, porosity), thermal (e.g. thermal diffusivity, conductivity, specific heat), mechanical (e.g. hardness, compressibility, elasticity, plasticity, friction, viscosity), electrical (e.g. conductance, resistance, dielectric properties) and optical characteristics (e.g. reflectance, transmittance, absorbance, contrast) (Rahman, 2009). Understanding the physical characteristics of a certain food is important for both increasing consumer acceptability and designing a correct technological process that is able to perfectly adapt to the specific product, minimising the undesirable changes while maximizing the process efficiency.

Water activity

In food and feed science, water represents a key aspect for defining the shelf life of each product. However, although moisture content can offer an important indication related to the status of the product, this parameter does not effectively inform about its stability and storability. The total water in a product may be bound to compounds or frozen, and thus unavailable to microorganisms and enzymes. Water activity (aw) on the other hand refers to the amount of water that is effectively free and therefore available for microorganisms and enzymes (Wynants et al., 2019). It is a thermodynamic property defined as the ratio between the vapour pressure of water in a food/feed matrix and the vapour pressure of pure water at the same temperature (Barbosa-Cánovas et al., 2020). Water activity of insect products is generally measured using commercial meters with hygrometer sensors that detect humidity in the headspace above the sample. Traditional methods, such as sealed containers with manometers, might also be used (Zhang et al., 2017). However, due to environmental variables (e.g. temperature, container material) and stabilisation time, these methods are discouraged.

Water activity may also be estimated using moisture sorption isotherms (Zhang et al., 2017), which are curves of equilibrium between moisture content and water activity, which allow, by knowing the moisture content and the temperature of the sample, to inversely calculate its water activity. However, as every product has singular behaviour, the water activity determined through this method will remain an approximate estimation. Differences due to insect species, products (whole insect, flour, extracted protein, paste, etc.), physical properties (e.g. different particle size), storage temperatures, containers and even batches should indeed be addressed (Sun et al., 2021).
Colour

Colour is the first sensorial parameter to be considered. It alone can form the first impression of a food, either attracting or repelling potential consumers (Kröger et al., 2022). Similarly, several animal species, such as chickens, tend to select feed based on the colour (Heuel et al., 2021b). The colour of insect-derived products is generally measured with a colorimeter, which must be properly pre-calibrated using a white standard. To ensure reliable and reproducible results, sample homogeneity and representativeness must be guaranteed. Analysis of pulverized fresh or dry material is preferred over that of whole insects. Caution is, however, advised as enzymes naturally present in insect cells may be released during milling, promoting enzymatic browning (Janssen et al., 2017a). Additionally, proteins may react with quinones naturally present in insects, forming melanins that further darken the product (Cacchiarelli et al., 2022). Colour measurement should be conducted promptly under well-controlled and standardised conditions. The exact time between milling procedure and colour measurement, as well as environmental conditions (temperature, presence/absence of oxygen, light exposition) applied during the milling-measuring time interval should be specified (Rossi et al., 2023). Multiple scans (three to six) across different areas of the same sample should be performed, and the average value reported (Kamau et al., 2020). Value recording should be taken at a 0° incidence angle (sample perpendicular to the colorimeter sensor), with scanning distance and area specified (Ojha et al., 2024). CIELAB colour space values obtained from the analytical device might therefore be used for determining specific physical properties such as browning index (BI), total colour difference (ΔE), chroma, hue angle, whiteness index (WI) and yellowness index (YI) (Cacchiarelli et al., 2022; Lenaerts et al., 2018).
pH and titratable acidity
pH is an important quality parameter in food and feed, affecting its stability in terms of microbial growth and enzymatic activity (Ojha et al., 2024). Low pH and high acidity are generally seen as beneficial for extending shelf life. However, insect products are naturally characterised by a close-to-neutral pH, making them harder to preserve (Ojha et al., 2024). As with any food and feed product, pH is measured using pH-meters, which should always be pre-calibrated and standardised with at least two standard solutions (usually pH 4 and pH 7). Accuracy and precision are crucial points in measuring pH. Regulatory bodies typically require accuracy of at least 0.1, and precision is ensured by repeating measurements – three repetitions are usually sufficient. Accuracy and reproducibility should also be ensured by measuring the pH at standard temperature (20 °C); if a different temperature is used, it must be specified. Alternative methods, such as colorimetric strips, are not recommended due to low precision and accuracy, making them unsuitable for research purposes. While pH measurement in liquids is straightforward, measurement of solid materials might require sample preparation. In such a case, insect material can be dissolved in distilled water (insect:water ratio = 1:10), and the suspension should be thoroughly homogenised for at least one hour at room temperature (Mancini et al., 2019; Ojha et al., 2024). After pH determination, the same solution may be used to determine titratable acidity (free acidity if determined on extracted fat). This refers to the amount of free acidic groups that are present within the solution and is measured by titrating the sample solution with a strong base. NaOH (or KOH) at 0.1 N is generally used, and the titration is complete when the pH of the solution reaches 8.1-8.2 or its colour turns pink in presence of few drops of phenolphthalein indicator (Klug et al., 2018; Purschke et al., 2017). Titratable acidity can be determined in both original sample solution and extracted fat (which needs to be re-solubilised in water miscible solutions such as toluene:alcohol (1:1, v/v) at ratio sample/solvent of 1:10) (Purschke et al., 2017). Titratable acidity, expressed as g NaOH/g sample, can then be calculated using the following equation:
titratable acidity (g NaOH/g sample)=(VNaOH∗NNaOH∗MWNaOH)/Wsample
Where VNaOH, NNaOH and MWNaOH stand for volume, normality and molecular mass of the titrant solution, while Wsample represents the mass (in g) of the sample contained in the titrated solution.
Techno-functional properties of insect protein

Protein is often considered the primary nutrient provided by edible insects. However, due to variations in insect protein composition and the effects of specific extraction and fractionation processes, protein fractions with significantly different properties might be obtained. Among these, techno-functionality is particularly important, as it directly affects the usability of insect proteins and their inclusion in traditional and/or novel foods and feeds. The aim of this section is to offer general recommendations for determining the techno-functional properties of insect protein. It should be specified that while these recommendations are generic and therefore valid for studies not considering specific food/feed applications, they might not always be suitable when specific applications with well defined conditions are addressed.

Techno-functional properties of protein can be classified in (Huamanı́-Perales et al., 2024): (i) hydration properties (i.e. properties depending on the interaction between protein and water: solubility and water absorption capacity); (ii) surface properties (i.e. properties depending on the interaction of protein with immiscible phases: foaming and emulsifying capacity); and (iii) interaction properties (i.e. properties related to protein-protein interaction: precipitation and gelation).

Protein solubility is defined as the proportion of insect protein soluble under specific conditions (López-Gámez et al., 2024). It is measured by mixing a certain amount of insect protein with distilled water and adjusting the pH with HCl or NaOH. Other solvents with predetermined pH values may also be used. The mixture is stirred to allow protein dissolution, then centrifuged, and the supernatant is recovered. Protein concentration in the supernatant is measured, and protein solubility is estimated as the ratio between protein content in the supernatant and total protein content. Although estimating protein solubility at different pH might be interesting, most of the food or feed applications require narrow pH ranges, suggesting that wide investigations might not be required. Measuring the protein solubility in distilled water or phosphate buffer at pH 7 (Edward et al., 2023) and a sample/solvent ratio of 1:10 (Ndiritu et al., 2019) might therefore be acceptable, unless specific application conditions are know and might therefore be defined. Regarding the temperature, protein solubility is usually measured at room temperature, and it should be considered as reference temperature for defining a standard method. On the other hand, different temperatures might be tested. For example, Bußler et al. (2016b) measured the protein solubility of yellow mealworm (YM, Tenebrio molitor) and black soldier fly (BSF, Hermetia illucens) at temperature ranges between 20 and 100 °C, observing a significantly higher solubility at 60 °C than 20 °C for YM, while no differences were noticed for BSF. Mixing time might also have important effects. Stirring time generally ranges between 30 min (Bolat et al., 2021) and 2 hours is applied (Purschke et al., 2018a). While no studies have directly evaluated mixing time effects, it is known that longer mixing can enhance extraction, with most of the effect occurring within the first hour (Mintah et al., 2020). Therefore, a mixing time of 1 hour, as used by Mishyna et al. (2019), is considered sufficient.

Water and oil holding capacity refer to the ability of proteins to absorb water or oil, avoiding their release (López-Gámez et al., 2024). Such properties are mainly influenced by the protein structure: protein rich in polar amino-acids on the surface show high water holding capacity, while those with non-polar amino-acids exhibit higher oil binding capacity. It follows that two different categories of methods can be used for defining these properties. The first one consists in applying the fluorescence sensitive probe ANS on the protein solutions dissolved in phosphate buffer and diluted at different concentrations (0.03–0.12 mg/ml). The fluorescence signal is recorded at 470 nm of emission over an excitation of 390 nm and the slope of the curve is calculated (Mintah et al., 2020; Mishyna et al., 2019). However, although this method provides valuable information on the protein properties, it is restricted to measuring surface hydrophobicity and not the effective techno-functional properties. Therefore, the second method should be carried out. Specifically, water absorbing and holding capacity is measured by mixing (5-30 min) a certain amount of protein (1 g) with a certain amount of distilled water (10 ml) (Bessa et al., 2022). The mixture is then centrifuged (≥3000×g, 10–20 min) and the pellet is weighted after discarding the supernatant (Bußler et al., 2016a; Mshayisa et al., 2022). A similar procedure but with oil instead of water can be used for determining the oil binding capacity. Different oils have been used, including canola oil, olive oil, sunflower oil and rapeseed oil. Use of different oils might however affect the results suggesting that standardisation is needed. Use of sunflower or palm oil is recommended, as they are the ones of the most common use in food preparations.

Foaming capacity refers to the ability of the protein to maintain air bubbles in suspension (López-Gámez et al., 2024). It is analytically determined by subjecting the protein in water solution (1%, w/v) placed within a graduated cylinder to high-speed homogenisation. Homogenisation time and speed should be carefully defined. So far, values ranging between 22 000 rpm for 5 min (Edward et al., 2023) and 2000×g for 2 min (Lee et al., 2019) have been applied. Homogenisation at 16 000 rpm for 2 min as applied by Zielińska et al. (2018) appears therefore to be a good compromise. To allow reproducibility, pH of the solution should be adjusted to 7 and protein concentration should be standardised. Specifically, while Zielińska et al. (2018) and Vanqa et al. (2022) used a concentration of 1% w/v, different concentrations were applied in other studies. The foam produced by the protein solution is therefore recorded as volume immediately after the homogenisation. Following readings, after 5, 10, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min can be performed to evaluate the foaming stability (Edward et al., 2023; Hall et al., 2017; Mishyna et al., 2019; Purschke et al., 2018b; Vanqa et al., 2022; Zielińska et al., 2018). Foam should be kept at room temperature during this time.

Emulsifying capacity refers to the ability of insect proteins to interact with oil-water interfaces (Mishyna et al., 2021). It is measured by adding insect proteins to a water-oil mixture, and measuring the volume of the emulsified layer, against the volume of the overall mixture (Kim et al., 2017). Amounts of oil and water should be the same and protein concentration within the water phase should be specified. Various protein concentrations have been used so far. Optimal concentration should be defined based on the protein solubility and the pH of the solution (Purschke et al., 2018a), although concentration of 1% (w/v) at pH 7 can be considered appropriate (Zielińska et al., 2018). Time and speed of the emulsifying operation should always be specified. Temperature might also directly affect the emulsifying properties of the protein (Lee et al., 2019). Correct estimation of emulsion capacity should indeed be carried out at room temperature, although use of different temperatures might be required. Presence of salts in the water phase should be considered as well (Kim et al., 2017), and use of distilled water should always be preferred. Similarly to oil holding capacity, the use of different oil might lead to significantly different results. Although each specific application might require different oils, standardisation may be reached by using sunflower or palm oil.

Emulsion stability could be measured after several times of incubation at room temperature. Following the method applied by Mishyna et al. (2019), emulsion stability can be evaluated after 10, 20, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min. However, in several studies, the words “emulsion stability” are attributed to the condition of the emulsion measured after heating at 80 °C for 30 min (Lee et al., 2019; Mshayisa et al., 2022; Vanqa et al., 2022). Though this method can be used for estimating the emulsifying properties at high temperature, it should not be used for emulsion stability, as technically uncorrected. An alternative method for measuring the emulsifying properties of insect protein has been proposed by Hall et al. (2017). In such a case, 50 μL of emulsion generated by mixing 3 ml of 0.5% protein solution dissolved in 0.1 M phosphate buffer at pH 7 with 1 ml of canola oil were diluted in 0.3% SDS solution and the absorbance was spectroscopically determined at 500 nm. Although such a method offers more reproducible results, it should not be applied. It is indeed longer and more complex and it measures the turbidity, which is not directly correlated with emulsifying properties.

Gelling properties are finally related to the capacity of the protein to aggregate and form three-dimensional networks (López-Gámez et al., 2024). They are generally measured through the formation of a gel: the protein in water solution is brought to a specific pH and a certain temperature is applied. Protein aggregation occurs and the ability of the protein to produce a stable gel is evaluated (Mishyna et al., 2021). Initial protein concentration, pH, heating time, gelling temperature and ionic strength of the initial solution appear to be the main variables affecting gelling properties (Kim et al., 2017; Yi et al., 2013). Correct determination of gelling properties should therefore always include the use of insect protein concentrations ranging between 1 and 50%. On the other hand, use of different pH might not be always required unless it is not a specific research question addressed by the paper. Production of gel from protein dissolved in distilled water at pH 7 should therefore be considered as a reference. Gelling temperature and time are usually set at 85 °C and 30 min, as originally described by Yi et al. (2013). Different time-temperature combinations have also been tested, although Mishyna et al. (2021) observed that most of the proteins from insects aggregate at 80 °C.
4 Insect-derived products as feed ingredients

Insect-derived products represent a valuable and sustainable resource for animal feed due to their high nutritional value, which includes substantial protein and essential amino acids, as well as energy, vitamins, minerals, and bioactive compounds. Current research is focused on determining the optimal inclusion levels of insect-derived products in the diets of livestock and aquaculture species, and recent studies have provided a foundation for establishing the maximum tested inclusion levels of insect-derived products that do not negatively affect animal performance. Moreover, research into feed safety aspects has recently been intensified to monitor the fate of putative contaminants in insect diets across trophic levels into human food products (Heuel et al., 2023). Lastly, a potential use of insect meals as feed additives (i.e. very low inclusion levels (0.05–0.2%)) aimed at improving bird performance, health and gut microbiota could also represent a promising strategy to incorporate insect-derived products into monogastric diets (Józefiak et al., 2018).

Regarding the inclusion of insect-based ingredients in poultry feed, Table 3 presents recommendations based on findings from the scientific literature. In evaluating insect-derived products for laying hen diets, researchers have primarily focused on the BSF (Athanassiou et al., 2024), exploring various forms such as live larvae, dried larvae, oil, and meal. Research findings indicate that supplementation with live larvae up to 10% and dried larvae up to 5% does not adversely affect growth performance or feed conversion ratio (FCR) in dual-purpose local breeds or laying hens (Bongiorno et al., 2022; Montalbán et al., 2024). In terms of BSF oil, a dietary inclusion rate of up to 3% showed no significant effect on body weight or FCR (Kim et al., 2022) and were shown to not negatively impact fatty acid profiles of eggs upon 2% inclusion (Heuel et al., 2021a). Furthermore, inclusion of BSF meal at levels up to 12% exhibited no detrimental impact on growth performance; on the contrary, it sometimes reduced FCR and feed intake (Nassar et al., 2023; Veldkamp et al., 2024). Although limited studies have tested inclusion rates exceeding 12%, initial results indicate a potential reduction in productivity at higher levels (Bejaei and Cheng, 2023; Khan et al., 2024). Higher inclusion levels appear to be possible for dietary complementation of organic feeds for layers (Heuel et al., 2021b) that tend to be comparatively poor in certain essential amino acids (Heuel et al., 2022a). In recent studies on broiler chickens, insect-derived products, including YM meal, have been evaluated (Table 3). The inclusion of 8% live or dry BSF larvae in diets significantly increased the final body weight of the birds, with live larvae also improving FCR compared to the control diet (Ipema et al., 2022). Higher levels of BSF oil, up to 9%, were tested in broiler diets rather than in laying hens, with positive effects on growth performance observed (Kierończyk et al., 2023). Similarly, the inclusion of 12% BSF meal improved both final body weight and FCR relative to control diets (Baderuddin et al., 2024; Sajjad et al., 2024), although increasing the BSF meal inclusion level to 16% led to reduced performance (Chobanova et al., 2023). This may not apply to slow growing broilers where inclusion levels of up to 20% turned out to be promising, yet strongly depending on the production and processing history of implemented BSF meals (Heuel et al., 2022b). Lastly, the inclusion of YM meal at a 10% inclusion did not have a statistically significant impact on FCR, however, it did result in a measurable increase in final body weight (Vasilopoulos et al., 2023).

The study of insect-derived products in aquaculture has a longer history compared to their application in other livestock species. This progression aligns with European legislation, which initially approved their use in the aquaculture sector before extending to poultry and swine. The main species studied include Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout in freshwater systems, as well as sea bream and sea bass in marine environments. Recent studies have primarily focused on low to medium inclusion levels of insect-derived products, likely reflecting the projected market expansion and the practical applicability of these products in animal feed. Inclusion levels of BSF meal exceeding 20% in Atlantic salmon diets resulted in a decline in growth performance (Fisher et al., 2020; Weththasinghe et al., 2022). 5 Insect-derived products in monogastrics
Design of experimental trials

The primary monogastric species for which insect-derived products have been tested include poultry, pigs, and fish. Experimental designs for these studies differ across species, particularly in the number of replicates and animals used. Based on a review of existing literature and personal expertise, the key aspects of these experimental setups are outlined below. Designing a research study that aims to assess the influence of insect-derived products in monogastric species follows the same principles already developed for the classical zootechnical trials focused on dietary interventions. In poultry, the scientific community overall considers 5 as the minimum number of replicates (represented by pens or cages (depending on EU or extra-EU countries, respectively)) that allows obtaining repeatable and reliable results, with at least 8 birds (showing homogeneous live weight (LW) and the same age, sex and genetic line or breed) being allocated to each replicate. In accordance with these scientific recommendations, most of the research dealing with the use of insect-derived products in poultry implements 5 to 8 replicates per dietary treatment, most of which farming 8-12 birds each (Beller et al., 2024; Biasato et al., 2018b; Dörper et al., 2024; Hartinger et al., 2021; Khan et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2022). Smaller groups or individual housing may be increasingly less relevant in a global commercial context but still have merit to answer specific academic questions and transferability to more applied settings (Heuel et al., 2021a, 2022a).

Differently from what has previously been highlighted for poultry, experimental designs related to the use of insect-derived products in pigs display more heterogeneity in terms of number of replicates and animals/replicate. However, most of the research dealing with the use of insect-derived products in pigs (mainly focused on post-weaning phase, as this is the most critical period in pig production) set from 4 to 5 replicates per dietary treatment, most of which farmed 4–10 piglets each (showing homogeneous LW, the same age and genetic line or breed and a male/female proportion close to 1:1 (Biasato et al., 2019; Boontiam et al., 2022; Håkenåsen et al., 2021; Song et al., 2024)). It appears also feasible to set 8 replicates per experimental treatment with a single animal inside (Malla et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2022), but translating the obtained results into the commercial scenario may be more challenging.

In fish trials, to ensure statistical reliability and reproducibility, a minimum of three replicates per dietary treatment is needed (Bellezza Oddon et al., 2024; Gai et al., 2023; Moutinho et al., 2023; Weththasinghe et al., 2022). This allows the assessment of variability between tanks and helps to draw robust conclusions. If resources permit, four or five replicates per diet allow increased statistical power, but three is typically the minimum for meaningful comparisons. Under laboratory or pilot conditions, there are no specific indications concerning the minimum number of fish to allocate in each tank as this depends on species, physiological stage, tank size, and the expected final size of the fish, which directly relates to the final stocking density. The initial homogeneity of fish weight also influences the number of fish required for the trial. When fish exhibit uniform body weight, a minimum of 10-15 fish per tank is generally adequate to identify growth differences while minimizing individual variation. However, a larger sample size is advisable when the batch has greater weight variability. To reduce this variability, it is recommended to grade the fish prior to stocking. Some indications are present in the Best Practise Sheets delivered by the Susinchain project and a useful guide is provided by the Feed Innovation Network (FIN – https://f3fin.org/).
Diet formulation guidelines

Formulating a diet for poultry, pigs, and fish that includes insects does not differ from the formulation strategies commonly adopted for the commercial diets available on the market, but the lack of product standardisation and availability highlights the importance of carefully following a step-by-step approach per each tested batch (and avoiding, as a consequence, the utilisation of nutritional databases or data extrapolated from the available scientific literature). In addition to evidence for strong compositional differences between different insect species, and likely processing-mediated effects on products from any given insect (see above), there is indication that both insect dietary regime as well as genetic background can strongly influence nutritional profiles of produced insect biomass (Sandrock et al., 2022).

As a first step, insect meals need to be nutritionally characterised (DM, CP, EE, ash, GE, chitin, essential and non-essential AAs, calcium, phosphorus, sodium, potassium, chloride, FAs) and standard digestibility trials have to be carried out to define their nutrient (DM, CP, EE, apparent metabolisable energy corrected for zero N retention (AMEn) – for poultry – digestible or net energy (DE or NE) – for pigs – and AA) digestibility, and apparent digestibility coefficient (ADC) for gross energy and essential AAs for fish (Bellezza Oddon et al., 2024; Crosbie et al., 2020; Schiavone et al., 2017). Regarding fish nutrition, it is essential to assess the potential presence of essential fatty acids, particularly omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, which are vital for the health and growth of certain fish species. The presence of antinutritional factors could also be important, even if so far no major ones have been identified in insects (Gasco et al., 2023). In addition, as insects can be fed different organic waste, the quantification of potential contaminants (e.g. heavy metals, mycotoxins) is important to ensure the ingredient is safe for animal consumption.

Secondly, for all species and physiological stage, a control diet mimicking the commercial feeds has to be formulated, to test the insect-derived products in practical, market scenarios. In parallel, inclusion levels of the insect meals or oils (and, as a consequence, the replacement levels of the standard protein and lipid sources, respectively) need to be defined in the light of the available literature, by testing novel inclusion percentages based on well-settled practical recommendations (especially for the insect meals – currently set at 10% (Biasato et al., 2023; Gasco et al., 2023)). Furthermore, formulation efforts have to be directed towards the main replacement of one, single protein (i.e. soybean meal or fish meal) or lipid (i.e. soybean oil or fish oil) source, to more easily attribute the observed outcomes to the inclusion of insect meals or oils, respectively. Nevertheless, given there exist ample variation, for instance, among types of fish meal traded in different regions, deriving from whole fish versus trimmings and/or being implemented in different production contexts such as conventional versus organic (Stadtlander et al., 2017), compositional differences of the original protein or lipid source will inevitably influence maximum possible substitution levels. Generally, moderate changes in the inclusion levels of the other raw materials are, however, sometimes unavoidable to keep the experimental diets nutritionally balanced among each other.

As a third aspect to consider, control and insect-containing diets need to be isonitrogenous, isoenergetic and isoaminoacidic (considering the digestible levels calculated above) to avoid creating some nutritional imbalances as underlying bias. Most of the above-mentioned studies correctly formulated isonitrogenous and isoenergetic diets (Biasato et al., 2018b; Khan et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2022; Malla et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2022), but very few authors only considered the dietary AA profile, taking into account the total AA amount (Hartinger et al., 2021; Heuel et al., 2022a), the essential AA amount (Boontiam et al., 2022) or the digestible AA levels (Beller et al., 2024; Dörper et al., 2024; Håkenåsen et al., 2021; Song et al., 2024).

Last but not least, the CP content of both the insect meal and the insect-based diets have to carefully be determined. In addition to the above-mentioned, chitin-related N-P conversion factors (Janssen et al., 2017b) that are specific for the different insect species and processing stages (meals or larvae), subtracting the N content of the chitin from the total N content of the insect-based diets has recently been recommended to not overestimate the dietary CP content (Beller et al., 2024). Further information on the assessment of the nutritional composition of larval biomass is provided in the specific Bugbook titled “Bugbook: common pitfalls and practical recommendations for chemical analysis of insect biomass” by Smets et al. (submitted).
Environmental setting

As with any scientific trial, the environmental and abiotic conditions must be thoroughly detailed in the Materials and Methods section. For poultry and swine trials, impacting factors include light photoperiod, ambient temperature, and relative humidity. In contrast, for fish trials, parameters such as water exchange rate, dissolved oxygen levels, water temperature, and light conditions are particularly important. Animal density is a key consideration across all monogastric species and should be selected based on the study’s objectives. To ensure applicability of the results, it is generally recommended to replicate densities reflective of standard industrial rearing conditions. During the trial, animal handling should be minimized to reduce stress, which could otherwise influence growth or other study outcomes. When handling is required for data collection or parameter evaluation, it should be performed under standardized conditions (e.g. consistent time of day, uniform methods, and fixed durations) to ensure reliability and minimize variability introduced by handling procedures.
Parameters to evaluate during growth trials

To monitor growth progression, periodic measurements of biomass are essential. For fish, this involves weighing the total biomass per tank on a regular basis (e.g. weekly or bi-weekly). In poultry or swine trials, individual or group weights (e.g. box weights) should be recorded at least at the end of each phase of growth. Biomass monitoring is particularly critical in fish trials, as feed quantities are typically adjusted based on a percentage of the tank’s biomass to account for growth over time. To minimize variation in growth due to feeding frequency, it is recommended to maintain a consistent feeding schedule, such as feeding 2–3 times per day at the same times. Monitoring feed intake is equally important, as reduced intake can directly impact growth rates. For accurate calculation of performance parameters, uneaten pellets should be recovered, and the exact quantity of feed distributed to each tank should be carefully recorded.

In order to assess if insect-derived products can be suitable for poultry, pig, and fish feeding, a classical, three-way approach is commonly adopted to obtain a full spectrum of information.

Firstly, it is essential to evaluate standard parameters associated with animal growth and production, including growth rates and productive performance, which vary across species as outlined in Table 7, the diet nutrient digestibility (DM, CP, EE, organic matter and AAs) and carcass traits (carcass yield and organ relative weights (broiler chickens, pigs, and fish)) (Beller et al., 2024; Biasato et al., 2018b; Dörper et al., 2024; Håkenåsen et al., 2021; Heuel et al., 2022b).

As a second aspect to consider, the implications for animal health must be considered as well. Within this scenario, it is mandatory to investigate the overall health status of the animals – by means of blood sampling (haematochemical profile and oxidative stress analyses) and animal necropsy (anatomopathological examination of the main organs) (Biasato et al., 2018b, 2019), but special attention should be paid to the role of the gut. Indeed, gut sampling is highly recommended to perform histomorphometric measurements (to evaluate the morphological development of the mucosa), histochemical staining (to identify the mucin types), 16S rRNA gene sequencing (to characterise the gut microbiota) and targeted gene expression analysis (to evaluate the mucin synthesis and inflammatory response) (Biasato et al., 2018a; Biasato et al., 2020; Colombino et al., 2021). Furthermore, metagenomics and metabolomics, as well as spectrophotometry – even if rarely performed so far – are expected to become of increasing importance to characterise the intestinal microbiome (Biasato et al., 2023) and digestive enzymes activity (Fiorilla et al., 2024), respectively. To assess the health status of fish, several readily available and straightforward indices can be utilized, such as the condition factor (K) and the viscerosomatic indices (Bellezza Oddon et al., 2024; Stadtlander et al., 2017).

Last but not least, the so-obtained food products (meat, eggs, and fillet) need to be qualitatively characterised in terms of chemical (proximate composition, AA and FA profile (meat, eggs, and fillet), and pH (meat, and fillet)), physical (colour (meat, eggs, and fillet), water losses (meat and fillet), haugh unit, yolk index, shell surface, volume and index, and albumen, yolk and shell ratio (eggs)) and sensorial (colour, odour, tenderness, juiciness, flavour and overall liking (meat), and odour and flavour) attributes (Dörper et al., 2024; Heuel et al., 2021a, 2022b; Schiavone et al., 2019; Shaviklo et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2022).

As a final consideration, carcass traits, health parameters and meat quality need to be assessed on at least 2 animals/replicate, choosing the animals whose live weight is closer to the average live weight of the replicate (Beller et al., 2024; Biasato et al., 2018b). As far as the egg quality is concerned, at least 6–8 eggs/replicate have to be collected (Dörper et al., 2024).
6 Live larvae and behavioural assessments

A few studies have been conducted on the use of insect larvae as environmental enrichment encompassing behavioural and welfare evaluations. The available literature pioneers this field of research and shed light, from a behavioural point of view, on the general effects observable in poultry (Bongiorno et al., 2024; Ipema et al., 2022; Star et al., 2020; Veldkamp and Van Niekerk, 2019). However, further research is needed to better explore live larvae provisioning in specific stages of the cycle and at a commercial level. To such an extent, integrating technology such as RFID-tracking would be advantageous to monitor birds’ activity and transition in litter/aviary systems. Overall, the birds’ response to the administered environmental enrichments depends on production type and genotypes (Ali et al., 2016), sex, age, rearing conditions (Purdum et al., 2020), personality, and individual experience (Ferreira et al., 2022).

For instance, most likely laying hens in commercial farms will not need “behavioural support” in terms of physical activity levels. On the other hand, if the larvae administration is properly offered within the rearing environment, it could keep them engaged and reduce damaging redirected behaviors towards conspecifics (e.g. feather pecking) (Star et al., 2020). Similarly, insect larvae could be helpful as preventive interventions against other unwanted behaviours such as piling. Considering broiler chicken production, insect larvae could offer interesting solutions to increase activity levels of such birds, contributing to the improvement of their welfare and health (Ipema et al., 2022).
7 Sampling time and sampling methods

Overall, if the anticipation and competition behaviours can revolve around the larvae administration event, the influence of larvae on the birds’ daily behavioural patterns needs instead to be deeper investigated. In this regard, video recordings need to be planned based on the research questions, considering when the behaviours of interest are explored. Various publications are available on daily behavioural activity of poultry and such studies can be considered as guidelines when approaching behavioural analyses (Mishra et al., 2005).

Ideally, a full 1-day observation of the birds repeated for 3 days is targeted to provide a robust dataset. On the other hand, time and resources do not always allow to carry out a comprehensive behavioural analysis, and priorities based on the most relevant behavioural aspects need to be defined (Daigle and Siegford, 2014). Although effective choices can cause limitations, they do not compromise the validity of the research as long as the methods are clearly explained. Moreover, the collection of the main behavioural characteristics offers a solid starting point in case no studies are available on a certain behaviour in a specific context.

Finally, the sampling method affects the representation of the behaviour analysed, but different options are viable based on the research questions and available time. If feasible, continuous observations that monitor the duration of a specific behaviour and the number of birds engaged in performing it, provide the most complete information. On the other hand, recording the duration of a behaviour is time consuming for the observer, increasing the error probability and possibly compromising accuracy and precision of the observations when several birds are active on the litter (e.g. laying hens in aviary systems). This represents an intrinsic limitation of behavioural analysis which needs to be acknowledged, and that explains why a good balance between precision-accuracy and the simplicity of the decoding is fundamental for reliable and efficient data collection.

Accuracy is defined as the ability to obtain the same result by repeating an observation over time (Menditto et al., 2007). In this way, the differences between treatments can be detected regardless of the differences in precision, which represents the ability to identify the actual number of behavioural instances in the population (Menditto et al., 2007). Although researchers should aim at keeping a high degree of precision, the ease of the video decoding procedure is fundamental as well to minimize the error probability over time.

For instance, in the event of analysing foraging behaviour, a choice can be made between the continuous observation analysis and a scan-sampling. The first method would better display the actual foraging occurrences compared to the second one, but the time necessary to decode a video with the first method would be significantly greater. Furthermore, the birds’ occupation of the litter changes over time, and it can be arduous to provide a reliable number of occurrences. Finally, in case the scan sampling is adopted, expressing the proportion of birds performing the behaviour on the total birds present in the observed area is fundamental to provide real information of the data.

Much detailed information about the sampling methods is provided by Bateson and Martin (2021) and Daigle and Siegford (2014). There is often more than just one approach acceptable and compatible with the research objective, and each one presents downsides and benefits. Researchers’ discretion defines which approach fits better the experimental design and the available resources for video decoding.
8 Ethogram assessment, observer training, and reliability check

The ethogram assessment represents a focal point while analysing behaviours. The description of the behaviour needs to be neat and can be customized based on the actual visibility of the chickens within the recorded environment. For instance, when recording a foraging behaviour from a vertical observational perspective, it will be arduous to understand whether the chickens are actually making contact with the ground. Researchers can choose to rely on the actual events in which the birds’ beak touches the ground, or may consider the inclination of the body towards the ground as a determinant factor, as long as it is consistent over time. One fundamental step in behavioural recording is reliability assessment (Bateson and Martin, 2021). A certain degree of subjectivity is involved whenever a behaviour is monitored, and the role of a solid ethogram and proper training is to standardize such observations. Intra-reliability concerns the capacity of the observer to repeat the same observation and achieve the same result over time. The inter-reliability measures instead the proximity between the observations performed by two or more observers. A good degree of reliability is considered above 0.80 and excellent over 0.90 and different statistical tests are available based on the nature of the analysed variable (e.g. continuous, count, etc.). The inter-reliability evaluation implies the reassessment of a portion of the observations by a second observer. In case a consistent amount of data needs to be extracted, an evaluation of inter-reliability on a weekly basis is suggested, so that correction and further training, in case needed, can be planned without compromising the entire data set.
9 Insect-derived products in ruminants
Design of experimental trials

Compared to research on monogastric animals the use of insect-derived products in ruminant nutrition is newer, so the number of available published papers on the topic is much lower than that available for monogastrics.

In ruminant nutrition research, it is a common practice to perform preliminary in vitro studies to test the suitability of new feed sources. In vitro methodologies reduce the length and cost of the trials, while also minimizing the number of animals to be used. These methodologies mainly consist in the incubation, typically with at least 3–4 independent incubation runs (statistical replications), and for a minimum of 24 hours, of the tested feed ingredient or diet (in duplicate or triplicate as technical replications) with rumen fluid and a buffer solution. The aim is to simulate the real rumen environment as realistic as possible. Ruminal fluid is obtained from a minimum of 4 donor animals, which can be either male or female (neither pregnant nor lactating) animals. These animals should be fed a diet that ensures standard rumen conditions and maintains a stable rumen microbiota population. In vitro studies allow researchers to evaluate the effects of the tested feeds on ruminal microbial populations, fermentation parameters (volatile fatty acids and gas productions), protein degradation, fatty acids (FA) biohydrogenation, and intestinal digestibility (Renna et al., 2022; Toral et al., 2022). Currently, some in vitro studies, focusing on assessing the suitability of using insect-derived products in ruminant nutrition, are available in the scientific literature (Hervás et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024b; Renna et al., 2022). Promising in vitro results usually lead to subsequent in vivo trials, which indeed give the most reliable data (Vinyard and Faciola, 2022).

To date only few in vivo nutritional trials with insect-containing diets have been performed, leaving substantial knowledge gaps to be covered. A possible reason for the limited research on this topic may be the stringent legislative framework that can be found in many countries worldwide, which mainly regards the use of processed animal proteins (including insect meals) in ruminant nutrition, as a measure to prevent and eradicate the potential risk of Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (Renna et al., 2023). However, research can still be conducted after approval by the competent authority.

In vivo experimental designs for ruminant animals may differ across species (i.e. cows, buffaloes, sheep and goats) and breeds, particularly whether dairy or fattening animals are considered. The overall minimum number of animals that should be employed for ruminant nutritional trials account for 6 animals per experimental group if the animals are allocated in individual pens, and 4 pens per treatment if the animals are housed in groups. However, the application of Latin square designs allows less animals (usually 4 or 5) to be used, but researchers should be aware that the overall length of the trial will be increased (Hristov et al., 2019). Even if papers have been published with the above-mentioned minimum number of animals and pens, it is always recommended, as a good practice, to perform a power analysis a priori to determine the exact minimum sample size required to achieve the aim of each specific trial.

Once the number of animals to be used in in vivo studies is defined, researchers need to carefully select the animals to be included in the trial. Experimental groups are usually arranged to contain an equal number of animals each. Moreover, groups need to be balanced in terms of animal-related parameters that could potentially influence the dependent variables considered in the trial. In the case of fattening animals, such parameters usually include genetics (i.e. breed or genotype), sex, age and live body weight. If the trial involves lactating animals, a correct group balancing needs additional parameters to be considered, such as parity (i.e. the number of lactations), stage of lactation, milk yield, contents of milk main constituents (fat, protein, casein, lactose, and urea), somatic cell count and, in some cases, even the FA profile of the milk fat (Lu et al., 2024a; Renna et al., 2020).
Diet formulation guidelines

The formulation of diets for ruminant animals is performed considering the species-specific nutritional requirements and follows the general guidelines provided by Scientific Organizations such as, among others, the Institut National de la Recherche Agronomique (INRA, 2018), the National Research Council (NRC, 2007), and the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM, 2021). Control diets should be used and, when possible, formulated to closely resemble commercially available diets, especially if the aim is to provide results applicable in a commercial context.

As already emphasized in the section “Insect-derived products in monogastrics”, given the high variability and poor standardization of insect-derived products (especially insect meals) that can be used for feed purposes, it is strongly recommended to conduct comprehensive chemical-bromatological analysis of the insect-derived product to be tested, as well as of the experimental diets destined to the animals. In this sense, as seen for monogastrics, feedstuffs and diets should be analysed for their contents of DM, CP, ash, EE, non-structural carbohydrates (NSC), and chitin, following the same advice previously provided for monogastric trials. Several studies demonstrated an overestimation of the CP content of insect meals when using the conventional nitrogen to protein (N:P) conversion factor of 6.25; for this reason, to allow comparisons among different studies, it is alternatively advisable (i) to report only the analysed N content rather than the CP content of the insect meal or (ii) to use, if available in published literature, appropriate N:P conversion factors for each insect species and processing stage (Janssen et al., 2017b). In ruminant nutrition studies, feed proteins should be further investigated for their degradability potential inside the rumen, by analysing the rumen degradable and undegradable protein fractions (RDP and RUP, respectively) (Licitra et al., 1996). When dealing with the dietary inclusion of insect meals, a characterization of their AA profile is also advisable. Ruminant diets usually contain high amounts of forages that are characterized by high fibre contents. A proper characterization of fibre in feedstuffs and diets destined to ruminants is, therefore, compulsory. The Van Soest’s fibre fractions (neutral detergent fibre (NDF), acid detergent fibre (ADF) and acid detergent lignin (ADL)) must be preferred over crude fibre (CF). While expressing fibre fractions results, researchers should clearly state if NDF is assayed with (preferred) or without a heat stable amylase, and if the results for both NDF and ADF are expressed exclusive (preferred) or inclusive of residual ash. When possible, but compulsorily when investigating the potential use of insect oils and full-fat meals (which contain high EE amounts), the lipid fraction of feed ingredients and diets should be characterized for a detailed FA profile, together with possible analyses on other fat-soluble bioactive compounds (e.g. tocopherols) (Nekrasov et al., 2022; Renna et al., 2022).

In ruminant diets, insect-derived products are usually tested for a possible partial or total substitution of other feed ingredients, especially if the latter are no longer considered environmentally, economically or ethically sustainable. Possibly, they could also be included as a supplement to the basal diet, especially if the aim is to obtain a specific effect (e.g. improved animal health or reduced enteric gases emissions). In all the cases, it is important to consider and report the given basal diet (especially in terms of its Forage to Concentrate ratio; F:C) for two main reasons: (i) unbalanced diets can cause metabolic disorders to the animals, and (ii) especially when considering the dietary inclusion of insect oils and full-fat meals, significant interactions with the basal diet may be expected (i.e. a contrasting effect in the case of high or low F:C ratio of the basal diet), as already seen for plant oils. The experimental diets should be isoenergetic and isonitrogenous, and the energetic value of the feedstuffs must be evaluated (preferably by using the metabolisable energy (ME) when dealing with fattening animals and the net energy for lactation (NEL) when dealing with lactating animals) (Hervás et al., 2024; Nekrasov et al., 2022; Renna et al., 2022; Toral et al., 2022).

Due to the scant information on in vivo effects of feeding ruminant animals with insect-derived products, currently, there are no clear recommendations on which inclusion levels should be considered as optimal for ruminant species. Therefore, it could be advisable to use the published inclusion levels as baseline and to test new inclusion levels (considering one ingredient at a time) without deviating from the nutritional requirements of the animals based on common dietary recommendations (e.g. the EE content of the diet should not exceed 5-6% of the total diet DM, as higher levels may negatively affect nutrient digestibility and alter the ruminal microflora (Renna et al., 2023)).
Environmental setting

As in any other nutritional trial, general information on where the trial takes place and on the environmental and housing conditions of the animals need to be taken into consideration and clearly stated. Thermoneutral conditions must be preferred, unless the feed ingredient is tested for its potential effects in mitigating cold or heat stressing conditions. Additionally, for lactating animals, it is also important to detail milking time and routine. All the procedures and handlings of the animals should guarantee the animals well-being and must be in compliance with the legislative framework of the country where the trial is carried out.
Parameters to evaluate during trials

As in monogastric animals, a three-way approach can also be adopted for ruminant species.

The first aspect involves monitoring growth progression and/or productive performance through periodic measurements. In ruminant trials, individual weights should be recorded on at least two consecutive days, both at the beginning and at the end of the trial (as a minimum), preferably at the same time of the day (before the morning meal is recommended). This approach helps avoiding the bias of weight fluctuations caused by the animals’ evacuations, which in ruminants can amount to several kilograms. To provide a more accurate representation of the metabolic activity of the animals, their metabolic body weight should be presented together with their live weight. Initial and final body weight measurements allow the estimation of the average daily gain (ADG) for growing and fattening animals. Feed intake needs to be constantly monitored as it can give an indication of the overall diet palatability (which is a fundamental aspect to be evaluated when testing new feed ingredients) and directly influences growth rates and production performance. When dealing with lactating animals, milk yield needs to be recorded constantly. Linked to the above-mentioned parameters (i.e. body weight, feed intake and milk yield) is the feed efficiency of the animals. Many different feed efficiency predictors can be found in published literature; however, the metrics that are considered the most suitable for ruminant species are the feed efficiency index (FEI) and the residual feed intake (RFI). The former is easier to calculate, as it only accounts for the recording of individual dry matter intake for a minimum of 21 days. Conversely, the RFI fits better with more complex conditions (e.g. lactating animals), but its estimation is also more complex, requiring data collection on individual daily dry matter intake and individual daily milk production, again for a minimum of 21 days. The collection of ruminal fluid from individual animals (using oesophageal probes, cannulated animals or at slaughterhouse), at least at the beginning and end of the trial (before the morning feeding and milking), allows the characterization of rumen microbiota, the evaluation of FA biohydrogenation, and the quantification of end products of fermentation and degradation occurring inside the rumen (NH3-N, VFA, gases). Regarding the gaseous end products of rumen fermentation, particular focus should be addressed to methane (CH4), due to the important role of this gas in climate change, but also as an energy loss for the ruminant. It is worth noting that available studies have pointed out the potential of insect-derived products to mitigate enteric CH4 emissions from ruminants, with such a positive effect being mainly imputable to lauric acid (C12:0), a medium-chain saturated fatty acids abundantly present in BSF fat. Since protein feed sources are usually the most expensive in a ruminant diet, evaluating nitrogen (N) balance (by comparing N intake and N excretion) can give a valuable estimation of the dietary protein utilization efficiency. Additionally, since insect meals seem to be mainly sources of rumen undegradable proteins (Renna et al., 2022), it is also advisable to evaluate the intestinal digestibility of the protein from insects. These analyses can give an indication on how much of the given dietary protein is effectively used by the animal to meet its nutritional requirements (Barrio et al., 2023; Fukuda et al., 2022; Renna et al., 2022; Toral et al., 2022).

The second aspect focuses on the potential effects of insect-derived products on animal health. Blood sampling, usually performed at the caudal or jugular vein, can be used to assess the effects of the diet on major nutritional parameters (such as non-esterified FA, cholesterol, glucose, triglycerides, urea, and β-hydroxybutyrate) and on the oxidative status of the animals, since both provide an overview of the animal health status and metabolic condition (e.g. the β-hydroxybutyrate concentration can be used as an indicator of hyperketonemia) (Benedet et al., 2019). Currently, there are no available studies that investigated the effects of dietary insects on ruminal and intestinal histomorphometric (length and depth of ruminal papillae and intestinal villi, area of absorption and intestinal layers development) and histopathological measurements (degree of inflammation of the gastro-intestinal tract) in ruminant animals. However, conducting necropsies on fattening animals at the end of the trial can enable the collection of ruminal and intestinal samples to evaluate the potential impact of insect-derived products on the integrity of the complex gastrointestinal tract of these animals (Romanzin et al., 2024).

The third aspect regards the quality of ruminant derived-food products (milk and derived products, meat). To assess its quality, milk needs to be sampled (following a complete milking) periodically and individually, paying attention at creating proportional composite samples of the morning and afternoon milking, according to milk production recorded per milking. Milk samples are then analysed for their main constituents, somatic cell count and FA profile, which are also able to give an indication of the health status of the animal, other than on milk quality. For example, monitoring milk fat yield can give an indication whether the administered diet can induce milk fat depression (Renna et al., 2013; Renna et al., 2020). Milk-derived products (e.g. butter, cheese, yogurt, etc.) could also be the target of the research; sampling and analyses may therefore be addressed to consider such dairy products other than milk, focusing on chemical (e.g. proximate composition, FA profile and pH), physical (e.g. colour, texture) and sensorial (e.g. odour, taste, flavour) quality (Drake, 2007; Renna et al., 2013). To assess carcass characteristics and meat quality, a minimum of 8 animals per experimental group should be considered at slaughter (also in this context a priori power analysis is recommended to define the minimum number of replicates to be used). Carcass evaluation focuses on weight, conformation and fatness, meanwhile meat quality assessment involves chemical parameters (e.g. pH, proximate composition, AA and FA profiles, vitamins, minerals), physical attributes (e.g. colour, texture), and sensorial (e.g. odour, flavour) aspects. It is advisable to conduct the analyses on the longissimus thoracis et lumborum muscle. Currently, there is a lack of comprehensive published literature on carcass and meat quality evaluations of insect-fed ruminants. For this reason, we suggest referring to standard protocols commonly used for meat trials (Destefanis et al., 2000; Di Stasio and Brugiapaglia, 2021).

As a final suggestion, we highlight the possibility to perform metagenomics and metabolomics analyses, as they help understand ruminal microbiota and digestive enzyme activities (Kenéz et al., 2015). However, no data is currently available for insect-fed ruminants so far.
